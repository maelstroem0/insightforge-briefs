Lead Story

US equities have confirmed a correction. The S and P 500 is eleven-point-six percent below its October sixth high. The Nasdaq Composite has lost its year-to-date gain and is now down two-point-seven percent in 2026. All eleven S and P sectors fell last week. The vix closed Friday at thirty-five-point-eight-nine.

The de-rating has moved past chips. Anthropic, which listed last week in the largest IPO on record, fell nine percent Friday. Its shares have given back part of the paper gains that made the deal a benchmark for AI-lab valuations.

Two forces sit behind the selling. The first is doubt about whether AI-infrastructure spending earns its cost of capital. The second is the Federal Reserve, which has started raising rates. Both are being repriced into the same multiple at the same time.

The read-through is broad. When the index leader stops responding to its own record results, the discount rate is doing the talking, not the earnings line. That shows up first in high-multiple software and semiconductor names, and then in the financing structures behind data-center buildouts.

It also shows up in breadth. Every sector lower in a single week is not a rotation. It's a beta event, and beta events get financed with margin.

There is a counterweight. With just over a month left in 2026, the S and P 500 is still on track for a year-to-date gain of about one percent. That's a thin cushion, but it is a cushion.

Two dates now carry the weight. The Fed meets December eighth and ninth. Micron reports December seventeenth. And Black Friday spending is the next read on the consumer. The core fact again: US equities have confirmed a correction, with the S and P 500 eleven-point-six percent off its October high and the Nasdaq negative for the year.

Second Story

Turning to Antalya. COP31 ended Sunday without a cover decision, after delegates failed to agree on language covering fossil fuels. Talks ran more than forty-nine hours past schedule — the longest C O P on record.

The sticking point was the draft. The EU, the UK, Panama and the Marshall Islands rejected the Antalya Pact because it omitted any reference to fossil fuels while keeping language that promotes carbon capture and storage. Saudi Arabia and Russia blocked any mention of transitioning away from coal, oil and gas.

COP31 President and Turkish Environment Minister Murat Kurum acknowledged the result was "very disappointing." He said more than ninety countries had signed a pledge to lift electrification to thirty-five percent by 2035. The conference's initial goal was to move global electrification from around twenty-three percent to that thirty-five percent level. It was dropped from the final text.

The finance side fared no better. Countries had requested two-point-eight billion dollars to help with damages. Only three hundred forty-two million dollars was available. At this summit, just one hundred forty-two million dollars in additional funding was pledged — far less than at other recent COPs. Developing nations pushed for a one-hundred-twenty-billion-dollar goal. It disappeared from the failed deal.

Here's why it matters for portfolios. The collapse leaves the pace of the energy transition, and adaptation finance, to national plans and non-U N coalitions. That's a conditional signal in both directions — for clean-energy capital spending and for fossil-fuel investment — because neither side now has a multilateral anchor to price against. Watch national plans, and watch whether the electrification pledge survives outside the U N process.

The Round-Up

France's AI minister says the US data-center model differs from Europe's, as a study found data centers paid only thirty-eight percent of the infrastructure costs tied to their growth in 2025 — three-point-one-two billion dollars of an eight-point-two-five-billion-dollar bill across four grids. Power demand from data centers has grown nineteen percent a year since 2000, while US grid capacity grew two percent. Voters rejected data-center projects in forty of forty-one local ballot measures this month.

Three L N G tankers bound for Egypt changed course in recent days — two to Europe, one to Asia — as Europe and Asia outbid Cairo. A single cargo now costs about one hundred million dollars, putting November's import allocation near one billion. Cairo asked for about ten cargoes this month, down from twelve in October, and deferred five of the fifteen it had contracted into 2027.

Tata Sons is weighing what would be India's largest listing, with board-meeting and E G M agendas on the matter under discussion, according to Livemint. No valuation, size, timing or structure has been disclosed, so the next observable test is the board outcome.

President Trump said the US will not allow its companies to build data centers abroad and threatened enormous taxes if they do, warning that China could seize control of such facilities. No legal basis or tax rate was supplied. The conditional read-through runs to data-center, power and semiconductor supply chains if capex is redirected onshore.

Germany's government is advancing a fuel price cap bill. Handelsblatt calculations show it could cut petrol and diesel prices by an average of five to seven-and-a-half cents per liter, and bind eleven to fourteen percent of the time, with motorway stations exempted under the draft. The bill is still in internal government coordination.

And Tata Steel agreed to buy twenty-six-point-four percent of A P L Apollo Tubes for one hundred fifty-five billion rupees, or one-point-six-seven billion dollars, with a mandatory open offer for another twenty-six percent that could lift its holding to fifty-two-point-three-eight percent. A P L Apollo will acquire Tata Steel's tubes and pipes business and Tata Steel Colors for a combined twenty-eight thousand crore rupees. Tata Steel shares fell more than five percent. A P L Apollo fell more than fifteen.
