Lead Story

French assets sold off Monday, and the sovereign risk premium took the hit. The O A T-Bund spread — French ten-year yields over German — widened nine point nine basis points to one hundred forty-seven point three. That's the widest in two weeks.

France's ten-year yield rose thirteen basis points to four point nine percent, on track for its largest one-day rise since late September. The German ten-year, the euro zone benchmark, rose four basis points to three point four five percent.

The move followed a weekend of nationwide protest and a 2027 budget that still hasn't passed. Nearly twenty-four hundred people were taken into custody, and about a thousand police officers were injured. Fuel shortages left hundreds of service stations dry.

Equities followed the bond market down. The C A C 40 fell between one point five and one point seven percent. B N P Paribas and Société Générale each dropped three percent. The euro weakened. The real ten-year yield in France has now moved above the US equivalent.

Ten-year yields are falling almost everywhere in the euro area except France. The consequence reaches past Paris. French sovereign premia set the funding benchmark for French banks and corporates, and a wider O A T-Bund spread tends to pull peripheral euro zone spreads with it. It also raises the discount rate applied to French earnings.

The variable investors are repricing is political — next year's presidential election, and a budget that hasn't passed. If unrest and fiscal paralysis persist, French bank equities and peripheral spreads are the pressure points.

There's a rating test this week. Moody's review lands Friday. Watch whether the O A T-Bund spread holds above one hundred forty-seven basis points, or retraces.

To restate the core fact: French ten-year yields rose thirteen basis points to four point nine percent Monday, and the O A T-Bund spread widened to one hundred forty-seven point three basis points, a two-week high.

Second Story

Elsewhere this morning, European corporate credit is repricing. Agefi reports that European corporate credit spreads have widened to their highest level this year. The same report puts the jump in German long-term rates at forty-five basis points, with European high-yield spreads widening by eighty points.

That's the risk-free leg and the credit leg moving together. This is single-source reporting, so treat the magnitudes as directional until index prints confirm.

The primary market has already responded. Several bond issuances were postponed amid elevated volatility, Agefi says. That ends a run of resilience in European credit.

The read-through runs to corporate investment and bank lending, and then to equity valuations. Higher funding costs push marginal projects back and lift the discount rate on European earnings.

The high-yield leg is where the signal is loudest. An eighty-point widening is a financing-cost event. There's no issuer-level data, so this is a market-wide repricing rather than a name-specific story.

The common input across Europe right now is the level of long rates. The German ten-year is the euro zone benchmark, so a jump there resets pricing across the bloc. German long rates and French sovereign premia feed the same corporate discount rate.

For equity investors, that changes the multiple you're willing to pay for a levered European business. For credit investors, it changes the entry math on new issue.

The sequence to watch is postponement, then repricing, then cancellation. If issuance windows stay shut, financing conditions tighten on their own. Until they clear, European credit spreads are a live input into valuation models on the continent.
