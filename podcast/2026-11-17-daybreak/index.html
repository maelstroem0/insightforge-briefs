Lead Story

Gold fell to a one-year low on Tuesday, with spot trading near thirty-eight hundred fifty-six dollars an ounce. That's a decline of about four and a half percent over the past week.

The pressure comes from two directions. The dollar index climbed to a nineteen-month high near one hundred three-point-four. And the ten-year Treasury yield pushed toward five-point-one-five percent. Both moves have compounded over recent sessions.

Market-implied pricing puts the odds of a quarter-point Federal Reserve rate hike in December at just under ninety-seven percent. Gold pays no coupon, so it competes with cash. When short-dated yields rise and the dollar strengthens, the metal's opportunity cost rises with them.

That's the channel running through bullion, gold miners and other dollar-denominated metals. For anyone holding gold as a hedge, the hedge is being repriced by the same force lifting the dollar.

This is a repricing of the discount rate. It isn't a judgment on inflation, or on the metal's long-run store-of-value case. The dollar and the front end set the price, and right now both are moving the same way.

Precious metals aren't the only asset class priced off the real yield. Long-duration equities, unprofitable growth and anything with a distant cash flow feel the same discount rate. Gold simply prices it fastest.

What to watch is the Federal Reserve's upcoming meeting minutes, and then the December decision itself. The minutes land first, and they'll be read for how firmly the committee is anchored to higher rates. Any softening in that language would be the first thing to change gold's arithmetic.

So the core fact stands. Gold is at a one-year low near thirty-eight hundred fifty-six dollars an ounce, held down by a nineteen-month-high dollar and a ten-year yield near five-point-one-five percent.

Second Story

Turning to energy. Brent crude fell three-point-five-five percent to ninety-two dollars and forty-four cents a barrel on Monday. West Texas Intermediate settled down three-point-five-eight percent at eighty-five dollars and thirty-nine cents.

The move followed the International Energy Agency's projection of a one-point-seven million barrel-a-day global supply surplus in the fourth quarter of 2026. The agency reversed its earlier deficit forecast, citing recovering Middle East exports and rising Russian production.

The I E A also reported that Russia's crude output rose to eight-point-seven-three million barrels a day in October, a jump of two hundred ten thousand barrels a day.

Goldman Sachs, in a Sunday note, said it expects Brent to average eighty-eight dollars a barrel in 2027, and to fall to seventy-eight dollars by December 2027 as the market returns to a surplus.

That's the tension in energy right now. The Strait of Hormuz remains disrupted, and yet the balance sheet points the other way. Supply is returning faster than demand is absorbing it.

For crude futures and energy equities, a surplus forecast compresses the risk premium that the Hormuz incidents had built in. Refiners' margins and producers' cash-flow assumptions key off the same balance.

The offsetting variables are OPEC+ policy and any renewed supply disruption in the Strait of Hormuz. Both are the reason the surplus call isn't a straight line.

So the number to hold onto: Brent at ninety-two forty-four after Monday's three-and-a-half percent drop, with the I E A now modelling a one-point-seven million barrel-a-day surplus for the fourth quarter.
