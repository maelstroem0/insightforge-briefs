Lead Story

The Strait of Hormuz is seizing up. Only four tankers transited the waterway, against a typical thirteen-ship daily average, after US strikes on roughly one hundred Iranian targets and Iran's missile retaliation.

Brent crude rose one-point-three percent to ninety-five ninety-one on Tuesday, up about ten percent on the week. W T I crude climbed near five percent to ninety dollars. The US ten-year yield hit four-point-eight-one percent — its highest in almost three years. And the Dow fell four hundred points.

The sequence is direct. On September first, American forces struck I R G C air defense, radar, maritime and mine-laying assets near the Strait. Iran answered with twenty-five ballistic missiles at US bases in Jordan. Half reached Jordanian airspace, ten were intercepted, three landed without casualties. Two Filipino seafarers died Monday on a Saudi tanker. Iran's military command warned of, quote, heavier, more widespread, and devastating responses.

President Donald Trump escalated on Truth Social. He said, quote, if the failed Nation of Iran retaliates for this very justified attack, they will be hit again at a much harder and higher level. He added that almost total control of the Hormuz Strait puts him in a much better position.

Here is why this matters beyond the oil price. Only forty ships carried millions of barrels through the strait Tuesday, and Energy Secretary Chris Wright says seventeen million barrels moved Monday. If the chokepoint stays this tight, energy inflation feeds directly into the inflation data the Fed is watching. Markets now price a September rate hike at roughly seventy percent. That's a stagflation shock — oil up, yields up, equities down, all at once.

The reprice is global. Japanese five-year yields hit a record. The vix jumped seven percent today, and Bitcoin broke below seventy-seven thousand.

So the watchpoints are simple. Brent through one hundred dollars is the first trigger. The ten-year through five percent is the second — that's where forced deleveraging becomes a live risk. And the transit count itself, every morning. Four ships versus thirteen is the number that proves this matters, and it's the number to check again tomorrow.

Second Story

Turning to trade, where the tariff war with Canada just went from threat to schedule. After talks collapsed on August twenty-first, Washington levied fifty percent tariffs on twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods. Ottawa will retaliate dollar-for-dollar starting September eighth — same twenty billion dollar basket, at rates from fifteen to fifty percent.

Prime Minister Mark Carney set the tone in public. He said, quote, when the Americans stop doing memes, stop throwing shade, stop trying to be tough and start being serious about having those discussions, we can have those discussions. For its part, the US trade representative's office was blunt in the other direction. Jamieson Greer said, quote, there are no negotiations happening on trade right now.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent framed the asymmetry this way. He said, quote, I don't think you can be in a tit-for-tat with someone who's thirteen times larger than you are. The arithmetic supports him — polls show fifty-seven percent of Americans oppose tariffs on Canada — but the White House is not backing off the schedule.

Bessent also used the moment to isolate Beijing. At the G20, nineteen of twenty members backed a statement against non-market export practices. Bessent said, quote, it is clear that the country with the world's largest and unsustainable current account surplus, the People's Republic of China, was the dissenter. China's surplus hit a record one-point-two trillion dollars in 2025.

The investor consequences are concrete. Bank of America expects the Bank of Canada to hold at two-and-a-quarter percent, with tariffs adding roughly three-tenths of a point to inflation and cutting the same from G D P. That's a rates divergence trade against a market still pricing cuts. Short Canadian-exposed industrials and the Canadian dollar is the clean expression.

The date to hold onto is September eighth — the retaliation clock is already running. And watch whether the Bank of Canada's language next week confirms what Bessent's G20 move signals: trade blocs are hardening, and pricing is following.

Markets Check

Let's get to the markets. Brent crude was up about seven percent as of today's tape. The US ten-year yield rose sixteen basis points to around four-point-eight, with the two-year up ten. The dollar index gained six-tenths of a percent. Gold fell nearly five percent — the real-yield trade beating the war bid. Equities: the Dow lost one and a half percent, the Nikkei dropped almost three, and the vix jumped seven. Bitcoin was down about three percent.

The Round-Up

Elsewhere — France. The O A T breached its alert threshold today, and pension economics are the fight. Prime Minister Lecornu backs scrapping the ten percent retiree tax allowance, worth five billion euros, but rules out touching small pensions. Full deindexation would save seven billion a year, and Economy Minister Lescure says the contribution of wealthier retirees, quote, merits asking the question. E C B hikes next week compound the squeeze into the 2027 budget — the spread trade stays on.

Australia grew two-point-one percent year-on-year in the second quarter, beating the one-point-eight consensus on private demand and mining exports. But July inflation runs at three-and-a-half percent, so the Reserve Bank stays on a tightening path. Consumption rose just zero-point-four percent — fuel prices are biting.

In AI infrastructure, SoftBank's S B Energy filed for a five-to-seven billion dollar Nasdaq IPO, ticker S B E. The numbers are striking: a three-point-two billion dollar loss on one hundred thirty-nine million of revenue, against a four-hundred-thirty-billion-dollar project backlog. Nvidia committed one and a half billion in shares and backs a ten-gigawatt Ohio campus powering OpenAI. The prospectus says the business is, quote, substantially dependent on OpenAI. Circular vendor financing, now in a prospectus.

The European Commission greenlit up to thirty-five billion euros in subsidies for eleven gigawatts of new German gas plants — operational by end 2031, hydrogen-ready by 2045, first bidding round this month. E E X chief Tobias Paulun warns against letting reserve plants cap electricity prices, citing a massive loss of investor confidence.

Meta settled youth-safety claims with twenty-nine states for eighteen billion dollars, booking a ten billion dollar charge this quarter. Morgan Stanley says the legal clearing event could let the AI product pipeline flow — agentic ad tools, new subscriptions, the consumer agent Hatch. Teens are only about one percent of revenue, so the overhang was worth more than the exposure.

And a quiet signal from Beijing. Xi Jinping met Iran's President Pezeshkian briefly and unpublicized on the S C O summit sidelines, weeks before Xi's planned Washington visit. Restrained Iranian oil exports to China would matter for the Hormuz math.

The Outlier

One number crossed a line it hasn't touched in thirty years. Japan's ten-year government bond yield reached three percent — the first time since 1996 — with the thirty-year near four-point-two and Governor Ueda hinting at a hike at the September seventeenth meeting. Here's the transmission: Japan holds two-point-four trillion dollars of overseas debt, and investors have already sold eighteen-point-seven billion dollars of it this year. That repatriation is a structural bid for the yen and a structural headwind for every long-duration bond market, including Treasuries. Watch the September seventeenth decision — a hike accelerates the drawdown.
