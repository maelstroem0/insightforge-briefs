Lead Story

CoreWeave's five-year credit default swaps are at eleven hundred forty-five basis points, and its stock is down more than thirty percent this month.

The ten largest AI companies hold eight hundred eighty-two billion dollars in cash between them — and they're still borrowing.

Goldman Sachs Research expects global data-centre power demand to rise one hundred seventy percent between 2025 and 2030, with more than sixty percent of that growth coming from the United States.

That demand has to land somewhere. US construction of new high-voltage transmission lines averaged seventeen hundred miles a year between 2010 and 2014.

Between 2020 and 2023, that average fell to three hundred fifty miles.

The funding side isn't waiting. Goldman Sachs projects global hyperscaler debt issuance of four hundred twenty billion dollars in 2027 — more than sixty percent above its estimate for this year.

The ten-year Treasury yield ticked up to five percent on Friday, and the thirty-year to five-point-three-three percent.

Earlier this month the ten-year had reached five-point-three-one percent, then backed off while AI bond issuance paused.

This is another leg of a repricing that's been running for weeks.

Two channels follow. If power is the binding constraint, the spending that matters shifts from chips toward transmission, transformers and generation.

Goldman Sachs expects aggregate utility capital plans to reach one-point-three trillion dollars between 2026 and 2030.

The second channel is credit. AI infrastructure firms front-loaded their raises to get ahead of mega listings and government borrowing, and the Treasury market has absorbed that supply.

Watch two things from here: whether CoreWeave's default swaps drag the wider neocloud complex with them, and whether the ten-year holds five percent when issuance restarts.

The core fact again — default insurance on the marginal AI landlord sits at eleven hundred forty-five basis points, while the transmission build runs at three hundred fifty miles a year against seventeen hundred a decade earlier.

Second Story

Turning to the Gulf. Houthi rebels say they struck Saudi Arabia's King Khalid International Airport in Riyadh, an Aramco facility at Yanbu, the Rabigh refinery, crude pumping stations and the East-West Pipeline.

The Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted three ballistic missiles and two drones.

The number that gives it weight: operations at Riyadh's airport were halted on October tenth after an attack that killed thirteen people and wounded more than three hundred.

Flights resumed Sunday, after a two-week closure — the deadliest attack in the kingdom in more than a decade.

The Houthis say they're answering Saudi operations in Yemen that killed seventeen people, and that the truce the United Nations brokered in 2022 has been shattered.

Earlier in this campaign, Saudi Arabia intercepted another Houthi missile, and President Trump has threatened new strikes of his own.

It's the latest step in an escalation that has already pushed Washington to order partial diplomatic evacuations from Saudi Arabia.

Both accounts come from the parties themselves, and neither has been independently confirmed here.

Why it matters: the target list now spans Riyadh's airport, an Aramco terminal, a refinery, pumping stations and a pipeline — a risk premium across energy, defense and shipping rather than a supply loss.

The airport is flying again, so the disruption is contained for now. The read-through depends on whether the campaign widens.

Watch Aramco's facilities and Red Sea shipping in the sessions ahead — that's where the next escalation step would show up first.

The core fact again: strikes claimed on Saudi oil infrastructure and Riyadh's airport, and a coalition that says it intercepted three ballistic missiles and two drones.

The Outlier

One more number. Indonesia began cutting natural gas exports to Singapore on Saturday — thirty-one million standard cubic feet a day, split between Natuna and Sumatra.

Singapore's Energy Market Authority says that's less than two percent of daily gas demand, so the immediate hit is small. The signal is the interesting part.

Singapore has built its power market on imported gas, and a supplier of last resort is now diverting volumes to its own grid. The prime minister has told suppliers to honour their commitments.
