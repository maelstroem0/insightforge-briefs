Lead Story

The thirty-year Treasury yield hit five-point-five-oh-one percent — its highest since June two thousand four. The ten-year reached five-point-two-two-three percent, last seen in June two thousand seven.

Fed funds futures traders are now pricing better than seventy-five percent odds of a quarter-point hike at the October meeting. A week ago, that was forty-nine percent.

The Treasury's own auctions agreed. A forty-four billion dollar sale of seven-year notes drew mediocre demand Thursday, after weak interest at Wednesday's seventy billion dollar sale of five-year debt.

Oil is doing the pushing. Brent closed at one hundred six dollars and sixty cents a barrel, gaining for a second day and extending this year's rally past seventy-five percent. The two-year note yield rose more than four basis points to four-point-nine-four-one percent, so the front end is moving with the long end, not against it.

Here's what that combination does. A front end and a long end rising together tighten financial conditions without another Fed move. The average long-term US mortgage rate is back at seven percent for the first time since early 2025.

That higher cost of capital hits the longest-duration assets first — growth equities, emerging-market currencies, credit spreads. It isn't a US story alone. Germany's ten-year is near three-point-six-zero percent, back to 2008 levels. Japan's ten-year sits at three-point-oh-eight, a world away from its negative yields in 2020.

The yield on a global bond index hit four percent for the first time since 2007. M S C I's World Index dropped zero-point-two-nine percent, and the pan-European S T O X X 600 fell zero-point-five-five.

Watch the October FOMC and the next C P I print. Holding above five and a half percent on the thirty-year is the level that keeps the pressure on.

So the headline again: the thirty-year at five-point-five-oh-one percent, the highest since 2004, and October hike odds above seventy-five percent.

Second Story

Turning to Washington. President Trump hosted Chinese President Xi Jinping at the White House on Thursday for a roughly three-hour meeting, the first day of a two-day summit.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says the tariff truce now runs to January tenth, past its November tenth expiry. Beijing has not officially confirmed that.

On Bessent's account, the extension keeps lowered US tariffs in place and holds off China's suspended rare-earth and critical-minerals export controls. The two sides confirmed their first AI talks and weighed a Board of Trade mechanism for non-strategic goods.

Bessent's statement is the only official timeline so far. No joint statement, no tariff schedule and no product list has been published.

The stakes are visible in the history. In Busan last year, Washington and Beijing agreed to scale back tariffs on each other's goods that had gone past one hundred percent in 2025. A Pew survey this year found nineteen percent of US adults hold a positive view of China.

For exporters, AI hardware supply chains and critical-minerals users, this delays a cliff rather than removing it. The suspension of Chinese rare-earth and critical-minerals controls sits inside the same window, so those supply chains get two more months of cover.

The two-day summit left the bigger disagreements on trade, technology and Iran unresolved, and it runs a second day Friday, so further readouts are possible. A published tariff schedule before January tenth is the next test. Until then, this is a timeline, not a framework.

So the core fact: the truce runs to January tenth on the Treasury Secretary's account, and Beijing has not confirmed it.
