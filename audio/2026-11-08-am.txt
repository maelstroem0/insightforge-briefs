LEAD STORY
The Nasdaq 100 fell six-point-four percent in one session, and two and a half trillion dollars of market value went with it.
That was Thursday, November fifth — the composite's worst day since April of last year. The Philadelphia Semiconductor Index dropped twelve percent to a six-month low. Nvidia fell nine percent. A M D fell seventeen. CoreWeave and Nebius, the neoclouds that rent out AI computing, fell ten and fifteen.
The trigger, as the market read it: Democratic victories in the midterms raised the odds of restrictions on the US data-center buildout that underpins the whole AI investment case. Add stretched valuations, and open doubts about the payoff on all that spending.
Joe Gubler, a portfolio manager at Causeway Capital, which oversees about seventy-six billion dollars, put it plainly. Quote — 'There are going to be losers, and not everybody who's investing all of this money in capex is going to be a winner.'
The consequence is a rotation, not a retreat. Growth stocks trailed value by ten percentage points in October — the widest monthly gap since 2001, one month after their strongest showing in more than a year.
The money went east. India's Nifty 50 gained eight-point-six percent last week. A Hong Kong gauge of mainland companies added five-point-two, its best week since March 2025. Foreign funds put a net one-point-nine billion dollars into Indian shares through Wednesday.
There's a physical channel underneath the multiple compression. Roughly thirty percent of the US data-center capacity expected to come online in 2028 could be at risk from construction pauses, as local opposition over power and water grows.
So hold onto the fact itself: the Nasdaq 100 lost six-point-four percent and two and a half trillion dollars in a single session, on a regulatory read rather than an earnings one. Watch semiconductor breadth and fund flows from here — not one index day.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Strait of Hormuz, where a deal is being shopped and not yet signed.
Qatar is mediating a phased plan. Under it, the US would immediately lift its naval blockade and release a portion of the eight billion dollars in frozen Iranian assets. Iran would admit I A E A inspectors, and reopen the strait on day two.
The stakes are measured in barrels. Ten-point-two-three million barrels a day of Gulf crude passed through Hormuz in October. Kpler senior crude analyst Muyu Xu puts about forty-two percent of that to China, and thirteen percent to India.
Prices are still paying for the risk. Brent traded above ninety-seven dollars on Friday, and US crude was around ninety. Crude had fallen more than sixteen percent over the prior five sessions on hopes of exactly this kind of off-ramp.
Rapidan's read is the other side of the trade: an eighty percent chance fighting resumes, and crude back above one hundred dollars if it does.
Note the status carefully. This is a proposal, mediated, not an agreement. Nothing has been signed, and the blockade is still being enforced.
The transmission runs through tanker rates, insurance, and the inflation math in importing economies — India and China first among them, given those volumes.
Watch whether the phased sequence survives contact with both capitals. A signed deal takes the risk premium out of crude quickly. A collapse puts it back faster.
The core fact again: Qatar is pitching a Hormuz reopening that trades a US blockade lift for I A E A inspections, and Brent is still above ninety-seven dollars while it remains only a pitch.
