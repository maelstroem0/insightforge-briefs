LEAD STORY
Jared Kushner and Steve Witkoff have left Ukraine and Russia without a peace agreement. That is the headline from a weekend shuttle that carried real weight and delivered none.
The numbers that matter are three hours and two capitals. The envoys sat with Vladimir Putin in the Kremlin for three hours, then made a first-ever visit to Kyiv. Putin reiterated his maximalist demands on territory. President Zelensky insisted on security guarantees — and said he expects the war to continue into winter.
Witkoff, speaking after the meetings, struck a positive note. He said, quote: "We've had a very meaningful discussion, substantive, important, and we are very encouraged and we look forward to continuing this as long as it takes." Zelensky was more guarded. He said, quote: "We very much hope that we will be able to reach agreements with our American partners, and we are counting on the support of our European partners if the war continues in winter — and this is how it appears at the moment."
Both sides called the talks constructive. But no deal was announced, and no breakthrough came on Donbas — the territorial question at the center of everything. The distinction matters. Encouragement is a tone. A deal is a document. This weekend produced only the first.
Why it matters: a longer war could keep European energy costs elevated, defense spending politically salient, and grain supply chains exposed to disruption through the coldest months. Those are potential transmission channels, not a measured response in today's markets. For investors, the absence of an agreement leaves an important uncertainty unresolved.
To be clear on status: these were talks, not terms. No deal was announced, and both parties remain divided on territory. The thing to watch is whether the U.S. side tables any follow-up framework proposals. Until then, a geopolitical risk premium could persist. The envoys left Kyiv with warm words — and no deal on Donbas.

SECOND STORY
Elsewhere, Ukraine is running a sustained drone campaign against Russia's economic infrastructure, according to the Wall Street Journal.
The targets are systematic: refineries, shipping terminals, and e-commerce warehouses. Kyiv's aim is to impose mounting costs on Russia's economy — striking the assets that fund and fuel the war effort rather than the front line itself.
This is a campaign, not a single raid. Refining capacity and logistics are important transmission channels. Disruption could tighten global fuel supply as heating demand builds. The extent of any shortage would depend on repair times, inventories, demand, and replacement supplies.
The transmission to a portfolio could run through fuel prices. If Russian refined-product exports fall, global diesel balances could tighten and crack spreads — the refiner's margin — could widen. That could support some energy businesses while raising input costs for airlines and transport. Any inflation effect would depend on how large and persistent the disruption becomes.
Pair this with the inconclusive shuttle talks and two risks sit alongside each other: no diplomatic breakthrough, and continuing attacks on economic infrastructure. Together, they could add support to energy supply-risk premia. The reporting alone does not tell us how much markets have priced in.
What to watch: Russian refined-product export volumes and diesel crack spreads. They are the cleanest evidence of whether the drone campaign is tightening global fuel supply — or merely scorching headlines.

These are comments on the diplomatic outlook, not an announcement of a new escalation. They keep geopolitical supply risk in focus for oil markets.

That puts Chinese volume directly against Western automakers' margins in third markets. Watch European tariff responses and O E M pricing disclosures.

Read-through for suppliers is mixed: smaller battery packs could trim raw material demand, while small-engine generators gain a revenue stream. Battery-supplier order books are the tell.
And a shift in who's picking stocks: retail investors are handing portfolio control to artificial intelligence, per the Wall Street Journal. Broader adoption could build demand for AI-driven wealth platforms and put pressure on traditional brokers and asset managers. Adoption speed is the open question.
