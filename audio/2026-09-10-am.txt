LEAD STORY
Brent crude has broken through a hundred dollars a barrel as a tanker war strangles the Strait of Hormuz. November Brent futures rose about six tenths of a percent to a hundred and one dollars eighty-four, up forty percent since the war in Iran began. The mechanics are stark. US forces destroyed five Iranian oil tankers on Tuesday. Iran struck ten ships near Hormuz and attacked two US warships. Houthi strikes damaged Aramco's Jazan refinery, which moves four hundred thousand barrels a day. Hormuz transits have fallen to roughly two million barrels daily, a fraction of normal. And the damage is measured in what's missing, not just what's priced. The International Energy Agency reports that as of July, eight point three million barrels a day of Middle East production remained shut in. Global inventories drew down sixty-nine million barrels in July, an average daily decline of two point seven million barrels. The costs are already reaching consumers. US gasoline is up about thirty-two percent from a year ago, averaging four dollars twenty-two a gallon. US diesel has exceeded two hundred dollars a barrel equivalent. The banks are repositioning. Citi lifted its third-quarter Brent forecast to eighty-six dollars from eighty, citing a longer-than-expected timeline for reopening the strait. A N Z raised its short-term forecast to ninety-five. Goldman has flagged a hundred-and-twenty-dollar risk scenario. This matters well beyond the energy complex. Crude at this level feeds directly into inflation expectations, which is why long-dated bond yields have moved in lockstep — and why energy importers across Asia are seeing margins and currencies squeezed at once. Watch three things from here: E I A inventory data, tanker insurance rates, and any signal of a negotiation to reopen Hormuz. The regime shift is priced in barrels until one of those changes.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the escalation behind the oil price. The I A E A board has referred Iran to the U N Security Council for non-compliance — the first such referral in twenty years. The resolution passed with twenty-three votes in favour, three against and eight abstentions. The agency cited its lack of access to facilities damaged in the war, and four hundred and forty kilograms of uranium enriched to sixty percent. That stockpile is weapons-grade adjacent, and no one can currently verify where it is. There's a second front opening underground. Satellite imagery shows a construction surge at Iran's Pickaxe Mountain site, buried a hundred and forty miles south of Tehran. The Pentagon has awarded an emergency contract worth one point two million dollars to test subterranean strike capabilities against exactly this kind of hardened target. Here's why the referral matters less than it sounds. Russia and China hold vetoes on the Security Council, which limits the probability of U N sanctions. The verification advisory from our sources flags that some of these figures remain unconfirmed — so treat the vote count and the stockpile number as reported, not settled. The price-setting channel here is military, not diplomatic. That means the risk premium sits in oil, tanker insurance and Gulf shipping lanes, rather than in any resolution text. It also means escalation risk is now symmetric. A referral this pointed gives both Washington and Tehran a fresh justification for the next move, and Gulf infrastructure is within range of everyone involved. Watch two things: I A E A access reports, and Gulf shipping insurance rates. If insurance reprices before oil does, that's your early signal that markets expect the next strike before the next negotiation.

THE ROUND-UP
Now the sweep. First, the US-Canada trade war deepens. After failed talks, Washington banned imports of Canadian spirits, dairy and motorcycles effective September twenty-ninth — replacing fifty percent tariffs. Ottawa retaliated with twenty-seven point six billion Canadian dollars in matching tariffs. US spirits exports to Canada have already fallen over seventy percent, and a fifty percent auto tariff is threatened for January. Across the Atlantic, French Prime Minister Lecornu promised CEOs a reduced corporate surtax — worth seven point three billion euros in 2026 — while holding a thirty billion euro savings target. But the growth forecast was cut to around half a percent, and the deficit goal slipped to five point three percent of G D P. Watch the O A T-Bund spread as the fiscal test. Japan: Bank of Japan board member Masu warned the central bank may need to raise rates quickly if inflation accelerates — a signal that matters for the yen and for every carry-funded trade exposed to a Japanese bonds yield spike. Diplomacy: per a Kremlin readout, Trump told Putin in a one-hour call that a swift end to the Ukraine war could restore full US-Russia trade and economic ties. No agreement was announced, and no market move can yet be attributed to it. Back to energy's second-order effects: the oil shock lifted yields globally and kept currencies subdued, with Citi seeing limited euro gains even against a hawkish E C B hike expected Thursday. In India, the renewables buildout has a structural hole — renewables now exceed forty percent of generation capacity, but over eighty percent of battery storage supply comes from China, with domestic manufacturing under one percent of a two-hundred-and-sixty gigawatt-hour pipeline. And the climate tape: August 2026 tied July 2023 as the joint-hottest month on record at sixteen point nine six degrees, one point six five above pre-industrial — reinforcing demand for adaptation infrastructure and raising litigation risk for fossil-exposed sectors.

THE OUTLIER
Here's the oddity: the US Treasury tripled its buyback operation to six billion dollars for ten-to-twenty-year maturities — and long yields rose anyway. The ten-year hit four point eight four percent, the highest since 2023, and the thirty-year rose five basis points to five point three percent. The transmission is simple: a buyback can recycle supply, but it cannot manufacture demand at auction. With oil feeding inflation expectations and heavy auction calendars ahead, fiscal credibility and inflation risk have merged into a single trade in long duration. Watch this week's auction tails and mortgage spreads for the next repricing.
