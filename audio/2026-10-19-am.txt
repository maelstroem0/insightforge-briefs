LEAD STORY
The debt behind the artificial-intelligence build-out is being repriced, and the equity market is following it down.
Beignet Investor's Meta-anchored Hyperion bonds have slid from above par in early July to just under ninety-two cents on the dollar. Oracle's Michigan campus debt has fallen to eighty-seven cents from par. A CoreWeave unsecured bond sits below eighty cents, with yields toward fifteen percent.
Per the reporting, investors stopped assuming AI revenue would arrive fast enough to service that debt. Power and construction delays, and soft demand signals, did the rest. This is a repricing of the funding channel, not a judgment on the model.
The equity leg confirms it. CoreWeave shares fell nearly twenty-five percent over the week. Nvidia gave up about two hundred seventy billion dollars of market value in the same stretch.
Why it matters is arithmetic. A neocloud that borrows at fifteen percent instead of eight doesn't build the marginal data center. Fewer builds mean softer orders for accelerators, memory and power equipment. And a higher discount rate lands on every AI-linked equity, whether or not it carries the debt.
The pipeline is already thinning. SoftBank's S B Energy mothballed a fifty-billion-dollar listing. It's majority-owned by SoftBank, backed by OpenAI and Nvidia, and it hasn't finished a single data center. It carries one hundred seventy-four billion dollars of capital needs against four hundred thirty billion of future contracts, most of them with OpenAI.
Anthropic, separately, can walk away from a forty-five-billion-dollar deal with Nscale if the start-up misses its financing and delivery dates.
Watch D R A M contract prices and credit spreads side by side. The first divergence between them is the signal. The core fact stands: the bonds are trading below par, and the equity is only now catching up to them.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's Foreign Minister Abbas Araqchi says the offer to reopen the waterway within seven days is still on the table — and within six, if Washington accepts Tehran's conditions.
Those conditions include lifting the US port blockade and the oil-export sanctions. By the reporting, talks have stalled.
The number that proves it matters: Brent settled at one hundred nine dollars eighty-six cents a barrel, up three-point-seven percent. W T I crude added more than ten percent last week. Before the war, the strait carried about a fifth of global oil and L N G trade.
The Revolutionary Guards reported tankers struck sea mines. That's the mechanism — not a threat, an observed event.
Why it matters: a chokepoint premium is now embedded in energy equities, tanker freight and marine insurance. Reopening depends on sanctions relief, and sanctions relief is a political decision in Washington. It isn't a shipping decision in Tehran.
So the premium decays on headlines and rebuilds on mines. For portfolios that cuts both ways. Integrated majors and freight owners carry the upside. Airlines, chemicals and energy-intensive manufacturers carry the cost. The insurer's reaction function matters more than the diplomat's.
Watch the next seven days against Araqchi's own clock. If the offer lapses without talks resuming, the market has to price a longer closure than it has so far. And watch refined products specifically — diesel, not crude, is where this war's damage shows first.
To restate it: Iran will reopen Hormuz within days if sanctions come off. Until they do, Brent holds near one hundred ten.

THE ROUND-UP
M S C I has proposed moving Indonesia straight to standalone status, citing insufficient progress on investability, opaque shareholding structures and suspected coordinated trading. Jakarta's benchmark fell eleven-point-six percent Friday — its biggest one-day drop in nearly three decades — and more than five percent Monday to a six-year low.
Goldman Sachs estimates a full removal could trigger roughly five-point-six billion dollars of passive outflows. Watch Jakarta's governance response, and the end-June decision calendar.
China's economy grew four-point-five percent in the third quarter from a year earlier, beating a four-point-four percent poll and up from four-point-three. The first nine months averaged four-point-six, inside Beijing's target range.
The detail is uglier than the headline. Real-estate development investment fell twenty-point-six percent in January through September. Retail sales rose two-point-six percent across the same period. Goods trade rose eighteen-point-eight percent to thirty-nine-point-nine trillion yuan. Exports are carrying this economy; households aren't.
Japan is weighing a corporate tax increase to fund defense spending likely above ten trillion yen a year — roughly sixty-three billion dollars — under security documents due by year-end. A rough estimate puts the possible outlay at seventy to eighty trillion yen, against about forty-three trillion projected for fiscal 2023 through 2027.
