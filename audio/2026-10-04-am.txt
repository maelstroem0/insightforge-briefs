LEAD STORY
The October rate hike is nearly off the table. C M E FedWatch data put the probability of a twenty-five-basis-point increase at the Fed's October twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth meeting at thirteen-point-eight percent — down from twenty-four-point-four percent before the report, and from roughly seventy percent on September twenty-eighth.
The trigger is the September jobs report. US employers added just twenty-nine thousand nonfarm jobs against an eighty-four-thousand Dow Jones consensus. The unemployment rate rose to four-point-two percent from four-point-one.
Under the surface it's softer still. About four hundred eighty-five thousand additional people were looking for work, pushing participation higher from sixty-one-point-six percent. August job openings were seven-point-one million, down from a revised seven-point-three million in July. Hiring held at five-point-two million in August.
The layoff side is calm. Challenger, Gray and Christmas says planned layoffs fell eighteen percent to forty-three thousand two hundred eighty-one in September, and announced cuts so far this year are down thirty-nine percent from the same stretch of twenty twenty-five.
Hiring plans rose to ninety thousand seven hundred eighty-seven in September from just twelve thousand three hundred twenty-five in August — but they're still twenty-three percent below a year earlier.
Inflation hasn't cooperated. August P C E rose zero-point-three percent on the month and three-point-four percent on the year; core P C E ran zero-point-two and three-point-zero. Consumer confidence fell six-point-seven points to eighty-one-point-nine. Consumer spending, about seventy percent of the economy, grew at a three-point-eight percent annual pace.
The read-through, if this pricing holds, favors short-duration Treasuries and gold, and weighs on the dollar. It doesn't solve the long end, where term premium rather than policy sets the price. The two-year yield sat near four-point-nine-four percent on September twenty-eighth.
Watch the October twenty-seventh and twenty-eighth FOMC meeting, and whether the two-year holds below that September print. And keep the core number: twenty-nine thousand jobs, and October hike odds at thirteen-point-eight percent.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Strait of Hormuz. Iran's Revolutionary Guard navy says it targeted at least seven 'violating' oil tankers in five days, according to Fars, with two tankers hit by explosions on Friday.
Iran tightened its grip on the waterway on February twenty-eighth, barring safe passage for vessels belonging to or affiliated with Israel and the United States, after joint strikes on Iranian territory.
The currency tells the same story. Iran's rial hit a fresh low despite a two-billion-dollar currency intervention. On an annualized basis the rial is down one hundred forty percent since the blockade restarted on July fourteenth. Official inflation ran about ninety percent year over year last month.
The transmission runs through two channels. One is a risk premium in crude, if these attacks are confirmed and sustained. The other is tanker insurance and freight, which reprice faster than the barrel does. Partly offsetting both, oil flows through Hormuz are recovering as Iran comes under growing strain.
There's no crude price move attached to this report yet. So treat it as a risk that's building, not a number that has printed.
The two facts sit side by side: a waterway restriction running since February, and a strike tempo that has now reached seven vessels in five days. Watch Brent, and whether the Guard confirms action against US- or Israel-linked shipping beyond these claims.
Restate the core: seven tankers in five days, a rial at a fresh low, and a blockade that restarted in mid-July.
