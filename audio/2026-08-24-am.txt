LEAD STORY
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent today unveils what he calls the most crushing economic operation ever mounted against Iran. He has promised the toughest sanctions in history. The war is six months old, and military strikes have not forced capitulation.
The new push is financial isolation, aimed at Iran's oil revenue and its trading partners. The goal is to cut the revenue that funds the war. The U.S. believes this financial wall will do what strikes could not. Roughly twenty million barrels of oil a day normally move through the Strait of Hormuz.
Tehran has now threatened to close that waterway to all shipments. Bessent says, "At dawn begins an economic D-Day — the single greatest financial offensive ever marshalled against an adversary." The Iranian side is not standing down. Mohsen Rezaei, secretary of Iran's Supreme National Security Council, warns that any country joining the restrictions will be treated as an enemy.
Brent crude already gained more than six percent last week. The early move today was lower. Brent futures fell one-point-three percent to about ninety-three dollars a barrel, and W T I crude dropped one-point-three percent to just under eighty-six dollars. The stakes are clear.
A full closure of Hormuz would take one-fifth of global oil supply and one-fifth of global L N G trade off the market. Energy-importing Asia and Gulf equities would feel it first. Tanker rates and crude are the obvious long side if the closure executes.
China buys more than eighty percent of Iran's seaborne oil, so enforcement there will decide how hard the sanctions bite. Pakistan's army chief is also in Tehran today. No confirmed attacks hit the strait in the past forty-eight hours. The press conference is set for one p.m. Eastern.
Watch Brent's one-hundred-dollar level if Tehran moves beyond rhetoric. The core fact is unchanged: Washington is shifting from military pressure to an economic offensive, and Tehran is answering with Hormuz.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the UK. Iran-linked hackers shut down a small power generator for four days in July in what appears to be the first successful cyberattack of its kind on a British energy facility. This is not just an infrastructure story; it is a portfolio risk. A spokesperson for the UK Department for Energy Security and Net Zero says:
"This story refers to an incident impacting a small-scale energy generator, and at no point was there a risk to the wider energy system. The UK has a highly resilient energy system. We work closely with the energy sector to protect infrastructure and ensure the highest security standards."
The attack followed Britain's decision to allow American defensive operations against Iran from UK bases. Iran's Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps had warned that any base used for aggression against Iranian territory is a legitimate target. The same Iran-linked group was tied to at least seventy-five compromised devices in a 2023 U.S. campaign.
Conservative energy spokesperson Claire Coutinho says the world is getting more dangerous, and Britain must prioritize energy security. The UK government had authorized those operations, and Tehran had threatened exactly this kind of retaliation. The market read is bearish for UK utilities and bullish for cybersecurity spending and cyber insurance.
The four-day outage was disclosed after the fact. The proof of concept is now public. Watch whether this accelerates backup power investment and critical infrastructure protection.
The core fact: a state-linked cyberattack took a British power asset offline for four days, and the government says the wider grid was never at risk. That is the tension markets must price.
