LEAD STORY
The European Central Bank raised its three policy rates by twenty-five basis points. It's the second increase of 2026, and it takes the deposit facility rate to two-point-five-zero percent from two-point-two-five, effective September sixteenth.
The main refinancing rate goes to two-point-six-five percent. The marginal lending facility goes to two-point-nine-zero.
The number that forced it: euro-area inflation rose to three-point-three percent in August, from two-point-nine percent in July. Energy inflation alone ran at fourteen-point-three percent.
The E C B framed the decision around energy-driven price pressures and an economy that has held up better than expected. It also revised its 2026 euro-area growth forecast up to zero-point-nine percent.
The lending channel is still transmitting, too. The annual growth rate of bank lending to firms rose to four-point-four percent in July, from four-point-zero percent in May and June.
Sticky energy inflation plus resilient activity is why the easing cycle markets expected this year has ended. The read-through is bearish euro-area duration and bearish rate-sensitive equities.
The periphery is feeling it as well. The spread between French and German ten-year borrowing rates has reached nearly zero-point-nine-four percentage points, the widest since 2012, as investors question France's fiscal trajectory.
The test from here is simple. If Brent stays above a hundred dollars, a third E C B increase stops being a tail risk and becomes the live question.
So, the core fact once more: the E C B raised rates twenty-five basis points, its second hike of the year, effective September sixteenth, with energy inflation at fourteen-point-three percent the reason.

SECOND STORY
Turning to energy. Brent crude for November delivery gained zero-point-six-two percent to one hundred one dollars and eighty-four cents a barrel. U.S. West Texas Intermediate rose one-point-zero-one percent to ninety-six dollars and six cents.
That's another settle above a hundred, after yesterday's breach. The cost of crude is now up forty percent since the war in Iran began, and Brent sits nearly thirty percent above its early-August lows.
OPEC says the demand side is cracking. In its monthly report, OPEC lowered its forecast for world oil demand growth in 2026 to three hundred eighty thousand barrels per day. That's the fifth straight downward revision.
U.S. data points the same way. Total products supplied, a proxy for American oil demand, averaged twenty-point-one million barrels a day over the last four weeks. That's down three-point-seven percent from the same period last year.
Put those together and you have a supply shock with a softening demand picture behind it. Higher crude lifts energy equities, tanker rates and refining margins. It squeezes transport operators and consumer input costs.
The watchpoints are U.S. C P I and whether tanker traffic through the strait normalises. Neither is close. So the underlying fact stands: Brent closed above a hundred dollars on renewed attacks, and OPEC cut its demand forecast for a fifth month.

THE ROUND-UP
A sweep across the rest of the brief now. Start with trade, where Washington reached for a weapon it has never used. On August twenty-second, the United States imposed a fifty percent tariff on twenty-seven-point-six billion Canadian dollars of goods.
The legal basis was Section 338 of the 1930 Smoot-Hawley Act, invoked for the first time. Canada answered with dollar-for-dollar counter-tariffs effective September eighth, and raised duties on U.S. steel and aluminium from twenty-five percent to fifty.
Next, sanctions. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said Thursday that a large bank will be sanctioned on Monday as part of the Iran strategy, and that Turkey's largest bank will also be closed.
That follows sanctions on the Dubai branches of Egypt's second-largest bank, which is believed to have given Iranians one-point-eight billion dollars of funds. Trade finance and correspondent exposure are the transmission.
In technology, South Korea revised its espionage law to protect semiconductor technology, according to The Japan Times, as Seoul moves to guard its chip lead. The law doesn't name China explicitly, and no penalties or enforcement timelines were supplied.
In energy, Qatar is in talks to buy L N G from U.S. suppliers under long-term agreements. The reason is that the Iran war has crippled its ability to supply its own customers, according to a single report from columnist Javier Blas. Volumes, durations and counterparties weren't disclosed.
And Gulf capital is moving into Germany. The U A E pledged forty billion euros of investment during President Sheikh Mohamed bin Zayed Al Nahyan's state visit. German and Emirati companies signed twenty-nine agreements worth nine-point-four billion euros.
The pledge covers AI data centres with up to one gigawatt of capacity, plus energy and technology. That's the full sweep — trade, sanctions, chips, gas and capital, all pulling in the same inflationary direction.

THE OUTLIER
One number explains why crude never reached the doomsday forecasts. China holds one-point-four billion barrels of strategic crude inventory, against eight hundred twenty-five million in the United States as of December.
When Hormuz closed and choked off twenty percent of global energy supply, analysts had forecast one hundred fifty to two hundred dollars a barrel. Paul Gruenwald, global chief economist at S and P Global Ratings, said China "kind of saved the day."
That stockpile is the damper on the crude risk premium. It's also shrinking — China's imports rebounded twenty-two percent in July and six-point-two percent in August. Watch those import prints: a faster pull removes the cushion.
