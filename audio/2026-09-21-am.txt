LEAD STORY
The Federal Reserve raised its policy rate by twenty-five basis points to three-point-seven-five to four percent on September sixteenth, under Chair Kevin Warsh. It was fully anticipated. It still repriced the world.
The number that proves it matters comes from fund flows. US funds drew sixty-four billion dollars in weekly inflows, a three-month high. Global emerging-market funds recorded their first outflow in ten weeks, at eight hundred seventy-seven million, per E P F R data collated by Elara Securities.
That second figure is the tell. The ten-year Treasury yield moved above five percent, and the dollar strengthened. Global investors now have a more attractive alternative in US fixed income — and that pressures capital flows toward emerging markets.
The transmission runs through the discount rate. Higher US yields and a firm dollar keep pressing emerging-market equity valuations. India is the cleanest test. The M S C I India index is down seven-point-five percent in 2026, and it still trades at seventeen times one-year forward earnings, a premium to Asian and emerging-market peers, according to Bloomberg data.
The policy room is thinner than it looks. The India-US ten-year bond yield spread sits near two hundred basis points, close to multi-decade lows and roughly half its long-term average. The Reserve Bank of India left its repo rate unchanged at five-point-two-five percent at its last meeting.
With that spread this narrow, the R B I's degrees of freedom are limited. So watch October rate-hike odds and the two-to-ten-year curve steepening. Rising neutral-rate estimates lift global discount rates, which is conditionally negative for long-duration equities and bond portfolios. The next test is whether that emerging-market outflow becomes a trend or stays a single week.
One more time, because this is the whole story. The Fed hiked to three-point-seven-five to four percent. Sixty-four billion dollars went into US funds. Eight hundred seventy-seven million left emerging markets — the first outflow in ten weeks.

SECOND STORY
Elsewhere this morning, oil is down for a fourth straight day — and the reason is supply recovery, not de-escalation.
The Houthis kept firing. Attacks on Saudi Aramco's East-West pipeline pushed the state energy firm to increase exports through the Strait of Hormuz. Saudi output recovered to just over four million barrels a day in September, from two-point-four million in August.
Brent crude fell one-point-six-six percent to one hundred two dollars and fifteen cents a barrel. West Texas Intermediate dropped one-point-eight-three percent to ninety-eight dollars and forty-six cents. Hormuz crude and liquefied natural gas flows hit a six-month high.
The chokepoint hasn't disappeared. Missile and drone attacks on Riyadh and Yanbu persist. What changed is the routing. Aramco recovered the barrels by moving them through Hormuz instead of the pipeline.
Why it matters: lower crude eases input-cost and inflation pressure for energy importers, from Europe to India to Japan. It also pressures refining margins, and it argues against the supply-risk premium that pushed Brent toward one hundred ten dollars a barrel. That matters for every energy importer's trade balance.
Goldman Sachs sees upside to one hundred twenty dollars a barrel if vessel attacks intensify, along with gas and refined-product prices. The same bank says that if exports return to normal, it expects oil back toward eighty dollars.
So the near-term path runs through whether tankers keep moving, not whether the shooting stops. Watch Hormuz shipping rates. Freight and insurance move before the barrel does — they'll tell you which of those two numbers is coming.

THE ROUND-UP
Starting with the trade file. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent and Chinese Vice Premier He Lifeng held about eight hours of talks in New York. They agreed to establish a US-China AI dialogue, with a proposed notification mechanism, and to operationalize a bilateral Board of Trade.
That's a process step, not a deal, and it lands before the September twenty-fourth Trump-Xi summit in Washington. Washington's suspension of heightened reciprocal tariffs on Chinese imports is scheduled to expire November tenth. Chip, semiconductor, critical-minerals and export-control names stay conditional on that extension.
Ukraine fired more than one thousand drones at Russia overnight, hundreds of them toward Moscow, on the final day of Russia's parliamentary election. Moscow's mayor, Sergei Sobyanin, said four hundred fifty were destroyed en route and the Gazpromneft-Moscow Oil Refinery was damaged.
Russia's defense ministry said it intercepted one thousand one hundred ten drones and downed eight cruise missiles. Across the wider Moscow region, two people were killed and twenty wounded. The refinery was already expected to stay offline until early 2027.
In France, average diesel hit a record two-point-four-zero-nine-seven euros per litre on Sunday, according to A F P data from more than eighty-seven hundred stations. That tops the previous record of two-point-three-eight euros set on April eighth.
The International Energy Agency says seven-point-six million barrels a day transited Hormuz in August — thirteen-point-one million below pre-conflict levels. The prime minister's office meets Monday on fuel aid.
Minneapolis Fed President Neel Kashkari told Fox News that inflation remains too high, and that it goes beyond oil prices. No rate level, no C P I print and no market reaction was supplied. The read-through is directional: it backs a higher-for-longer rate path.

THE OUTLIER
One number worth sitting with: the thirty-year Treasury at five-point-three-four percent. That's the long end repricing neutral — the rate where borrowing neither restricts nor stimulates growth. Economists are marking that level higher, because economies are proving resilient to hikes, and markets now price sixty percent odds of another increase in October. Higher neutral means higher global discount rates, and long-duration equities and bond portfolios feel it first. Watch the two-to-ten-year curve for the steepening.
