LEAD STORY
France's ten-year government bond yield has broken above five percent for the first time since 2002. The yield on the benchmark OATs climbed eleven basis points on Thursday, and held around five-point-zero-three percent on Friday.
That's the highest French ten-year borrowing cost in nearly a quarter of a century. Behind the move: nationwide cost-of-living protests that drew four hundred ten thousand people and one thousand three hundred fourteen arrests. Moody's has kept France at Aa3, but with a negative outlook.
The government still targets a deficit of five percent of G D P in 2027, down from a projected five-point-four percent in 2026. That gap is the whole story for euro-area sovereign risk. French banks and domestically exposed equities carry the first-order exposure — the C A C 40 is down four and a half percent over the past month.
An intervention from Christine Lagarde earlier this month hasn't capped the move. The spread between French and German ten-year yields is the line to watch, and it hasn't narrowed. Higher long-end yields tighten conditions for households and corporates without a single E C B decision.
Note what this is and isn't. It's a repricing of French credit risk, not a change in policy. The E C B hasn't moved, and the budget hasn't changed. What changed is the price investors demand to hold French duration.
For a portfolio, the channel is straightforward. French sovereign yields are the reference rate for French banks, French utilities and French corporates. When the reference rate rises, the discount rate on every domestic cash flow rises with it.
France's growth outlook is already thin — one forecast puts 2026 growth at just zero-point-three percent, and 2027 at zero-point-six. Watch the O A T-Bund spread, and watch whether Moody's negative outlook turns into a downgrade. The ten-year French yield is above five percent for the first time since 2002.

SECOND STORY
Turning to energy. Saudi Arabia has partially reopened Riyadh's King Khalid International Airport after a Houthi attack this month killed thirteen people and wounded more than three hundred. Flights remain limited.
The Saudi-led coalition says it intercepted and destroyed twelve missiles and two drones launched toward the kingdom on Sunday, including a ballistic missile and two drones targeting the Riyadh region.
Here's the number that matters. Brent settled at one hundred sixteen dollars and seventy-two cents a barrel — a six-month high. That's a gain of three-point-one percent on Friday, and six-point-two percent for the week, a third consecutive weekly gain. W T I crude advanced two-point-three percent for the week.
Now, what hasn't happened. There's no confirmed loss of Saudi export volumes. The Houthis claimed fresh strikes on the airport and on Saudi Aramco facilities. The coalition said the Houthis carried out twenty attacks on the airport between October first and eleventh. The United States has ordered non-emergency government employees to leave Saudi Arabia.
So this is a risk premium, not a supply loss, and that distinction sets the read-through. Refined products and freight carry the tighter transmission. Airlines and fuel-intensive industries carry the cost. Every week Brent holds these levels, the inflation arithmetic for importers gets worse.
Watch whether the airport returns to full operations, the tempo of Houthi attacks, and whether the campaign widens beyond aviation and energy infrastructure. Brent's six-point-two percent weekly gain is the number to hold onto. The risk premium is priced — and it's still there.
