LEAD STORY
September US inflation came in hotter than economists expected — and the Fed's pushback set the tone, not the print. Core prices rose three tenths of a percent on the month, against two tenths expected.
On the year, core ran at two-point-six percent, against two-point-five forecast, with headline inflation at three-point-seven, a tenth above consensus. That combination lifted October Fed rate-hike pricing to fifty percent.
Fed officials then urged patience, and the market moved on that guidance. The dollar index had touched an eighteen-month high. It slipped a tenth of a percent to a hundred two point oh six.
The US ten-year Treasury yield fell to a three-week low of five-point-one-two-six-seven percent, and the thirty-year stood at five-point-four-seven-nine-five. New York Fed's Williams said Tuesday that one more increase in 2026 may still be appropriate.
October is now close to a coin flip between a hike and a hold, and that is a repricing of the front end. Higher front-end hike odds alongside softer long yields and a softer dollar is an unusual combination.
The stakes: the expected policy path is the discount rate on every long-duration asset in the book. Higher hike odds bind hardest on the assets furthest out on the curve of expected cash flows.
Currencies read it too. The Australian dollar fetched sixty-nine and a half US cents, down three tenths of a percent, after September employment there unexpectedly slid.
So the market's hike odds and the Fed's own guidance are pointing in different directions. Watch whether the ten-year holds below that three-week low, or resumes its climb on the next inflation print.
The core of it: core inflation beat consensus, October hike odds doubled to fifty percent, and the Fed is publicly pushing back.

SECOND STORY
Turning to energy. The International Energy Agency says the global diesel squeeze will run well into 2027, as the Iran war keeps disrupting supply. The number that proves it: global diesel exports fell seven hundred thousand barrels a day year on year last month.
The I E A attributes a one-point-three-million-barrel-a-day loss to Russia and the Middle East. It says wholesale diesel exceeded two hundred dollars a barrel in Singapore in mid-September.
In Europe and the United States, wholesale diesel reached all-time highs around two hundred twenty dollars a barrel. US diesel hit six dollars a gallon for the first time last month.
Diesel is the fuel the industrial economy actually runs on — trucking, freight, construction, farming. Persistent tightness there feeds straight into transport and input costs, and from there into headline inflation.
It also complicates the rate picture, because this is a supply-side price the Fed can't smooth away. On the supply side, President Trump said he struck a deal with Russia's Vladimir Putin for three hundred thousand metric tons of diesel immediately.
The I E A's own balances point the other way for crude. It sees overall oil demand falling two-point-six million barrels a day in 2026, and supply declining five-point-eight million barrels a day this year, before reversing to a rise of seven-point-seven million in 2027.
So the tightness sits in the barrel that matters most, and the relief being cut is Russian distillate. The diesel fact, once more: exports down seven hundred thousand barrels a day, and the I E A sees no cover before the back half of 2027.
Watch whether that Russian volume actually lands, and whether G7 releases take the top off the spread.
