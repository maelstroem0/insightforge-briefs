LEAD STORY
The twenty-year Treasury now yields more than the thirty-year. That inversion is the sharpest read yet on term-premium stress in the world's benchmark curve.
The twenty-year rose thirty-seven basis points since Chairman Kevin Warsh's August twenty-eighth Jackson Hole speech, to five-point-five-five percent Friday — its highest since June two thousand four.
The thirty-year touched five-point-five percent Thursday and again Friday, also its highest since June two thousand four. The ten-year ended Friday little changed at five-point-one-seven percent.
It's up sixteen basis points on the week and fifty basis points since Warsh spoke, and it traded as high as five-point-two-three percent during Friday's session. This is the second week of a global bond selloff.
Demand is the tell. At the September fifteenth twenty-year auction, the government paid five-point-four-two percent to sell all thirteen billion dollars of bonds on offer.
The drivers stack up: heavy government borrowing, a wave of AI-related corporate debt issuance, strong economic data, and elevated energy costs — all competing for the same capital.
Mortgage News Daily puts the average thirty-year fixed mortgage rate at seven-point-five percent Friday. Car loans, credit cards and business loans reprice off the same curve.
Bloomberg's Markets Pulse survey of one hundred seventy-three participants found more than half expect the thirty-year above six percent by the end of twenty twenty-six.
J P Morgan Asset Management estimates a geopolitical resolution that pushed oil down to seventy or eighty dollars a barrel would retrace one-third to one-half of the recent selloff.
So the long end is pricing policy risk and energy risk at once. The next tests are auction demand and the October Fed decision.
The core fact again: the twenty-year yields more than the thirty. The long end is the global discount rate, and it's still climbing.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Strait of Hormuz. Iran offered the United States a plan to reopen the waterway within seven days, contingent on Washington's acceptance, according to regional reports.
President Trump rejected it, and per those same reports he is preparing to restart bombing. Seven days was the first concrete reopening framework put on the table.
China was warned directly in the exchange. The message, as relayed: helping Tehran is, quote, totally unacceptable.
Washington hasn't published the exchange, and no timeline for a renewed proposal and no venue for talks were disclosed.
The stakes are logistical. A closed chokepoint keeps a war-risk premium in crude, in diesel and in shipping insurance.
It also removes the Gulf's main practical alternative for barrels that can't transit Hormuz, which is why crude has stayed bid through the standoff. Diesel and freight insurance sit downstream of that.
France, separately, says it will deploy troops, radar and air-defence assets to protect Saudi Arabia's Yanbu terminal on the Red Sea — the export hub for the kingdom's east-west pipeline.
Yanbu has faced renewed Houthi attacks, and the deployment extends Paris's previously discreet defence cooperation with Riyadh. European defence primes pick up a sustained Saudi-theatre demand signal from it.
For bondholders this matters as much as for oil. A credible reopening framework is the largest single disinflationary catalyst available to the long end right now — and it is further away this morning than it was yesterday.
The core fact stands: Iran offered seven days, Trump said no, and the strait stays closed. Watch for a revised Iranian proposal, and for any third party willing to broker a venue.
