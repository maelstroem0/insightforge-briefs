LEAD STORY
Saudi Arabia shut its East-West pipeline after drone attacks launched from Iraq struck the Riyadh and Medina regions on Thursday, damaging pump stations and injuring people.
The line has moved four to five million barrels a day — four to five percent of global oil supply. It's now offline while the Strait of Hormuz stays largely closed.
Saudi Arabia also reported to OPEC that its crude production plunged last month, sinking to the lowest levels since 1990. Two outlets for Saudi barrels, effectively gone at once.
Oil climbed past a hundred dollars a barrel this week. Brent settled at a hundred four dollars sixty-one cents Friday, after peaking near a hundred eight. Average US diesel prices topped six dollars a gallon for the first time Friday.
That's the channel that matters. This is a supply story before it's an inflation story, and the East-West line was the bypass that kept Saudi barrels moving around Hormuz.
Take away the bypass and the spare capacity it represented, and every barrel travels farther, costs more to insure, and arrives later.
For equities, that lands on rate-sensitive sectors first. Higher crude feeds headline inflation, and the Fed meets next week with a hike already priced near ninety percent.
Refining margins sit on the other side of it. Diesel above six dollars a gallon is a crack-spread story before it's a consumer story.
What to watch is narrow. Whether the East-West pipeline restarts, and whether Red Sea transits through Bab el-Mandeb resume at anything close to normal volume.
No demand destruction is documented yet, and no direct equity transmission is confirmed. This is a supply shock still looking for its ceiling.
The core fact again: Saudi Arabia shut a pipeline that has carried four to five percent of global oil supply, after drones launched from Iraq struck it Thursday.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the other thing on the table. US consumer prices rose four-tenths of a percent in August from July, and three-point-four percent over the past year.
Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, rose three-tenths of a percent month on month. Consensus was two-tenths. That single tenth is the story.
The so-called supercore measure, excluding energy and housing services, rose half a percent in August and was up three percent from a year earlier.
Traders in federal funds futures now see roughly an eighty-five to ninety percent probability of a quarter-point hike at next week's meeting. The Fed has held its policy rate at three-and-a-half to three-point-seven-five percent since January.
The University of Michigan's Surveys of Consumers showed its sentiment index tumbling to forty-seven-point-eight in early September from fifty-one-point-seven in August.
Average hourly earnings, in a separate report released Friday, increased just three-point-one percent over the year. Wages are not the driver here. Energy and services are.
That split matters for how you hold risk. A hike into rising energy costs compresses equity multiples, and it does it hardest in the long-duration, rate-sensitive parts of the market.
It also puts the burden on next week's meeting to explain itself, because the inflation print and the growth signals are pointing in different directions.
The number to hold onto: core C P I came in at three-tenths of a percent against two-tenths expected, and futures now price a hike near ninety percent.
