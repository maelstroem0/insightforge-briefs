LEAD STORY
US forces struck two Iranian rocket launchers on Larak Island on Sunday — the first American strike on Iran in a month. Brent crude jumped more than two percent to ninety dollars and thirty-one cents a barrel. A US official confirmed the strike, saying, quote: "Islamic Revolutionary Guard Corps forces were observed preparing to launch rockets with sea mines into Strait of Hormuz." CENTCOM spokesperson Captain Tim Hawkins called it, quote: "limited, precise action against I R G C minelaying forces posing an imminent threat."
Iran answered within hours. Ballistic missiles targeted US bases in Jordan. Jordanian air defense intercepted all eight, with two people killed. President Trump escalated on Truth Social, writing: "Kharg Island being blown to smithereens!!!" — a direct threat to Iran's main oil export terminal.
Here's why this matters. The Strait of Hormuz stays closed, and before this war it carried about twenty percent of global oil. Weekend transits limped along at roughly five commodity vessels a day. Every headline about mine-laying is now a direct bid under Brent and tanker rates, and a direct tax on Asian crude importers and Gulf risk assets.
There is one ceiling worth marking. A classified Pentagon assessment has concluded that prolonged large-scale operations would degrade US readiness elsewhere, and senior military leaders have warned Defense Secretary Hegseth against extending the war. That internal resistance tempers the escalation premium — for now.
So the trade frame is simple. Brent is above ninety dollars. A strike on Kharg Island is the scenario that takes it past a hundred. Watch for mine-laying headlines out of Hormuz — that's the trigger that reprices everything from freight to equities in one session. The core fact, once more: US forces hit launchers on Larak Island, Iran answered in Jordan, and the Strait remains shut.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the other price on Monday's tape — the price of money. Fed Chair Kevin Warsh has ended the Powell era of forward guidance, and markets spent the weekend repricing the September decision. Hike odds jumped from thirty-five percent before his Jackson Hole keynote to fifty-eight percent after it. The two-year Treasury yield rose six basis points to four-point-three percent, and the ten-year climbed to four-point-seven-two.
Warsh recommitted to the two percent P C E target — currently running at three-point-seven — and refused to guide markets on future moves. In his words, quote: "we should not indulge a regime in which market participants are looking primarily to the Fed for their next trade." The structural shift is stark: data must now justify a hold, not a hike.
The repricing spread everywhere. Gold fell three percent to forty-four hundred and fifty dollars. Nvidia dropped four-point-six percent. The Nikkei lost two-point-two percent. And the yen broke through one-sixty per dollar — a level that puts Tokyo's intervention desk on alert. The dollar strengthened broadly; E M currencies are under pressure.
Positioning-wise, the front end is the cleanest expression of this regime change. Navy Federal's Heather Long frames the path: "A hike probably won't come in September, but it will by October or December." Watch the pre-September C P I print. A hot one locks the hike and forces unwinds in crowded long-duration, gold and tech trades. That's the story again: Warsh ends forward guidance, and September hike odds sit at fifty-eight percent.
