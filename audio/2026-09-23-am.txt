LEAD STORY
Oil moved first today, and it moved lower. Brent for November delivery fell three-quarters of one percent to ninety-eight dollars and fifty-one cents a barrel. West Texas Intermediate fell about one percent to eighty-nine dollars and fifty-nine cents.
That follows Tuesday's settle, when Brent closed below one hundred dollars for the first time since September eighth.
The trigger is diplomacy. US and Iranian delegations met for roughly three hours at the United Nations in New York, their first session since June. Saudi Arabia also restarted its East-West pipeline to the Red Sea.
And the barrels kept moving by sea. Around fourteen million barrels of crude were loaded onto seven supertankers in the Gulf on Sunday, according to tanker-tracking data, as Aramco lifted exports through Hormuz.
That is the war premium leaving the price. Cheaper crude feeds into transport costs, freight rates and headline inflation expectations, and it does that faster than any statement out of a negotiating room.
One corner of energy went the other way. Natural gas futures posted their biggest one-day gain in six weeks, settling up four-point-five percent at two dollars and ninety-six-five per million British thermal units.
For equities, the relevant channel is the discount rate. Softer energy supports the inflation path, and that matters with traders pricing a fifty-three percent chance of a quarter-point Fed hike in October.
Nothing is settled. No agreement was signed. Iran's offer to reopen the Strait of Hormuz within seven days remains conditional on the US easing pressure, and a breakdown puts the premium straight back in.
Watch the range rather than the rhetoric. Brent's ninety-seven-eighty to one-hundred band is where you can see whether de-escalation is actually being priced, or merely hoped for. The core fact stands: oil fell as US and Iranian officials met for the first time since June, and Saudi crude kept loading through Hormuz.

SECOND STORY
Turning to Washington, where Xi Jinping arrives today for his first US state visit since 2015. His meeting with President Trump is set for Thursday.
Expectations are deliberately light. There's no Chinese chief-executive delegation travelling with him, and the agenda runs to extending the trade truce, AI, rare earths and Taiwan.
The leverage sits in a fourteen-billion-dollar US arms package for Taiwan. It remains on hold, and Trump has previously described it as a negotiating chip.
Goods have already been moving. According to US Department of Agriculture data, China has imported three hundred forty thousand tonnes of American soybeans. In September, the two sides held consultations on a framework for reciprocal tariff reductions potentially covering thirty billion dollars of products from each side.
Why it matters is the risk premium. A tariff-rollback framework would support China-exposed equities and pull supply-chain risk premia lower. Failure keeps export-control risk and Taiwan-delivery risk elevated.
Beijing is also balancing the AI race. It's determined to compete there, and AI safety sits on the agenda alongside the truce, which puts two very different files in the same room.
Watch the communiqué, not the choreography. The official readout on tariffs and Taiwan is the only observable deliverable. Without one, this stays a truce of convenience rather than a settled relationship.
