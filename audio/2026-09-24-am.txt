LEAD STORY
The US ten-year Treasury yield rose to five-point-one-one-three percent Wednesday — its highest level since July of two thousand seven. The move was close to fifteen basis points in a single session, and it came from three directions at once.
S and P Global's flash US composite P M I printed at fifty-eight point four for September, its strongest reading since July of two thousand twenty-one. A seventy-billion-dollar five-year Treasury auction drew weak demand at the same moment oil prices pushed higher.
And C M E FedWatch odds of another quarter-point hike in October now sit near sixty-eight percent, after the Fed lifted its policy rate to three-point-seven-five to four percent last week.
The repricing is not American. Japan's ten-year Japanese bonds yield touched a thirty-year high of three-point-zero-five-five percent. Equities absorbed the hit Wednesday: the S and P 500 fell eight-tenths of a percent, the Dow lost seven-tenths, and the Nasdaq dropped one-point-one-three percent.
Here's why it matters. The ten-year is the discount rate under every long-duration asset — equities, property, infrastructure, private credit. Above five-point-one percent, it compresses valuations in rate-sensitive sectors and raises corporate refinancing costs.
It also firms the dollar, which tightens financial conditions well beyond US borders. Front-end yields collect the hawkish premium; the long end carries the fiscal and inflation risk premium on top. Both are rising together, and that combination is the uncomfortable one.
The weak five-year auction is the clearest evidence that demand for duration has limits right now. That keeps duration and rate-sensitive equity exposure on the back foot while the front end stays supported.
Watch services inflation into Fed communications, the October FOMC meeting, and the next C P I print. The core fact stands: the US ten-year at five-point-one-one-three percent is the highest since July two thousand seven.

SECOND STORY
Also this morning, the O E C D raised its consumer-price projections compared with June for every Group of Twenty economy except China and Saudi Arabia — and said the Fed will hike again.
Separately, Fed Governor Michael Barr said inflation is running above the central bank's two percent target and is not clearly trending toward it in a timely way.
Barr said the Fed will likely raise rates again to counter persistent inflation, after it hiked last week for the first time in more than three years. Market pricing points to the next move in October.
Fed's Barkin said this week that one hike may not be enough. That's the same signal the bond market sent Wednesday, and it's one story: the policy reaction function has not turned.
Why it matters. If the O E C D's inflation call and Barr's framing hold, the front end stays anchored high through year-end, while the long end keeps its fiscal premium on top.
The dollar keeps its carry advantage, a headwind for emerging-market currencies and dollar-funded borrowers. For equities the transmission is the discount rate, and Wednesday's selling was the first pass at repricing it.
Note the divergence in that outlook: China and Saudi Arabia were the only Group of Twenty economies where price projections were not raised.
The O E C D also flagged weather-related inflationary risks, which reach food and energy first. The next observable tests are the October FOMC decision and the C P I prints before it.
The short version: the O E C D sees inflation lingering across nearly every major economy, and a Fed governor says another hike is likely.
