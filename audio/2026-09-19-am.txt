LEAD STORY
The Federal Reserve raised interest rates by a quarter of a percentage point on Wednesday, to a range of three-point-seven-five to four percent, its first hike since 2023.
The ten-year Treasury yield held above five percent, its highest since July 2007. The two-year reached four-point-seven-four-one percent, its highest since July 2024.
Federal Reserve Chairman Kevin Warsh's committee flagged more hikes in the coming months. Market pricing puts the odds of an October twenty-eighth move at sixty percent, and that number is rising.
One analysis using the Taylor Rule puts the entire cycle at just fifty basis points. So the market and the rulebook do not agree on how far this goes.
Fed leaders forecast inflation back at two percent by 2029.
The transmission is the discount rate. When the ten-year sits above five percent, every long-duration cash flow is discounted harder.
Long-duration Treasuries and rate-sensitive equities are where that pressure shows up first. The week agreed: the S and P 500 finished slightly lower, while the Nasdaq was the only major US index to gain.
The reach is global. A five-handle on the US ten-year sets the discount rate for European credit, emerging-market equities and long-dated infrastructure alike.
Crude above one hundred dollars complicates the Fed's arithmetic. It is tightening into an oil shock rather than out of one.
For an equity investor, that combination is the whole story: multiples face a headwind from rates even when earnings hold up.
The Fed meets again on October twenty-eighth. The market's own odds say that meeting is live, and the ten-year is the variable that matters most until then.
The core fact bears repeating. The Fed moved to three-point-seven-five to four percent, and the ten-year is holding above five.

SECOND STORY
Turning to energy. Saudi Aramco has told at least two European refiners they will receive zero crude allocations for October.
The reason is physical. An attack on Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline shut a critical Red Sea export route, and barrels are being redirected through the Strait of Hormuz toward Asia.
Roughly sixty million barrels are shifting to Asian buyers through Ras Tanura.
The prices tell the story. Brent traded around one hundred four dollars and thirty cents on Friday, Dated Brent topped one hundred thirty, and Europe's diesel benchmark exceeded two hundred dollars a barrel.
T T F gas reached ninety-two dollars and ninety-five cents.
Zero term allocations leave European refiners buying in the spot physical market at those premiums. The barrels are not gone. They are heading east instead of west, and Europe pays the difference.
Emergency stockpiles are absorbing part of it. I E A members have released more than three hundred million barrels since March.
That has not closed the gap. Global observed inventories are still five hundred seven million barrels below where they stood when the war began.
The draw has averaged two-point-eight million barrels a day over six months.
The read-through runs through European refining economics. Crude is getting scarcer in the Atlantic Basin and cheaper in Asia, and diesel is where the shortage is most visible.
Fuel costs then spread outward into transport, industry and household budgets across the continent.
The next tests are pipeline repair timing and whether disruption in the Red Sea or Hormuz deepens. Either would firm the physical premium again.
Aramco's zero allocations to Europe are the fact. Sixty million barrels heading east is the consequence.
