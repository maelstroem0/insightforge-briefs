SECOND STORY
Turning to Washington. President Trump hosted Chinese President Xi Jinping at the White House on Thursday for a roughly three-hour meeting, the first day of a two-day summit.
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says the tariff truce now runs to January tenth, past its November tenth expiry. Beijing has not officially confirmed that.
On Bessent's account, the extension keeps lowered US tariffs in place and holds off China's suspended rare-earth and critical-minerals export controls. The two sides confirmed their first AI talks and weighed a Board of Trade mechanism for non-strategic goods.
Bessent's statement is the only official timeline so far. No joint statement, no tariff schedule and no product list has been published.
The stakes are visible in the history. In Busan last year, Washington and Beijing agreed to scale back tariffs on each other's goods that had gone past one hundred percent in 2025. A Pew survey this year found nineteen percent of US adults hold a positive view of China.
For exporters, AI hardware supply chains and critical-minerals users, this delays a cliff rather than removing it. The suspension of Chinese rare-earth and critical-minerals controls sits inside the same window, so those supply chains get two more months of cover.
The two-day summit left the bigger disagreements on trade, technology and Iran unresolved, and it runs a second day Friday, so further readouts are possible. A published tariff schedule before January tenth is the next test. Until then, this is a timeline, not a framework.
So the core fact: the truce runs to January tenth on the Treasury Secretary's account, and Beijing has not confirmed it.
