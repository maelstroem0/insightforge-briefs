LEAD STORY
France's ten-year borrowing cost came within a whisker of five percent on Thursday, touching 4.96 percent in the session — its highest since 2002 — before easing as the 2027 budget was presented.
The benchmark O A T jumped sixteen basis points on Wednesday, and it's up more than a hundred basis points since the start of the year.
The O A T-Bund spread — French ten-year yield over German — reached nearly 160 basis points at the end of last week. Both numbers matter. The level is what France pays; the spread is what it pays relative to Germany.
The budget proposes 54 billion euros of effort, including 43 billion euros in new measures, to bring the deficit back to five percent of national output.
That arithmetic lands on a debt stock of 3,595.5 billion euros at the end of June — 119 percent of G D P. The deficit is running at 5.1 percent of output. Student protests and a budget deadlock have intensified concern over both the numbers and the politics.
Servicing that debt is projected to rise to roughly 100 billion dollars in 2027.
France has more than one trillion dollars of debt coming due by 2030, and next year it's set to sell a record of about 380 billion dollars into a market where once-reliable sources of demand have evaporated.
The stated implication is pressure on French duration and on OATs versus bunds.
The market's question is whether that 54 billion euro effort narrows the spread, or whether fiscal and political risk keeps the long end bid for yield.
Watch the auctions, and whether the ten-year holds near five percent or the spread pushes past 160 basis points. For now, France's ten-year is back at the doorstep of five percent, and the budget is the test in front of it.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the policy side now. The International Monetary Fund is urging governments to rein in spending as bond yields climb.
Managing Director Kristalina Georgieva warned of very tough political choices for officials trying to combat soaring yields.
She voiced concern about one number in particular — the ten-year Japanese government bond yield, which she says has reached its highest level in about thirty years.
Georgieva also flagged high long-term rates in Japan and the United States.
No quantified fiscal targets and no yield targets were attached to the warning.
Sovereign funding costs are the pressure point here, and restraint is the Fund's answer.
That lands at an awkward moment. Long-end yields across major markets are elevated, and governments keep issuing into that market.
Japan is the clearest case the I M F names. A ten-year yield at a three-decade high is a funding-cost repricing, not a rounding error.
The transmission to portfolios runs through duration. The longer the bond, the more a fiscal warning matters to its price.
The Fund's logic is that spending restraint is what relieves long-end yields. Without it, the pressure persists.
So watch the auctions. Tokyo and Washington both fund at the long end. The test is whether this call changes demand at those sales, or shifts policy guidance.
Two anchors, then: restraint as advice, and a Japanese ten-year at its highest in roughly thirty years. The next checkpoint is whether the long end keeps climbing after the warning.
