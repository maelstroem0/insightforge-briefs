LEAD STORY
SpaceX has closed roughly forty billion dollars in financing for AI chips and related data-center equipment, disclosed as a binding commitment in a regulatory filing Monday.
The number that proves it matters sits right beside it. Capital expenditures ran near twenty billion dollars, against six-point-nine billion a year earlier.
The structure is a lease. An Apollo-managed entity buys Nvidia's Vera Rubin chips and leases them to SpaceX for about five years. Elon Musk says the callable debt arrives in the first half of 2027.
Musk also says more than two hundred neoclouds face what he calls guppy-level death, as debt markets shut out companies without revenue outside AI.
Why it matters is the split it exposes. Investment-grade borrowers with diversified revenue can still finance compute at scale. Companies whose only asset is a data center and a G P U order book are being repriced by their lenders.
That gap runs through credit spreads, through G P U delivery schedules, and through the valuations of every listed AI landlord. For Nvidia, the read-through is demand financed off someone else's balance sheet.
For equity investors, it's the difference between owning the chip supplier and owning the lease. Watch two things from here.
First, whether any of this lease migrates onto the balance sheet, because that changes the leverage picture. Second, whether AI-related debt issuance keeps clearing at scale through the fourth quarter.
The core fact bears repeating. SpaceX has locked in roughly forty billion dollars of G P U capacity through a five-year lease, and its chief executive is publicly warning that the smaller end of the AI cloud industry will not survive the same financing test.

SECOND STORY
Turning to Tokyo. All sixteen respondents in a C N B C survey expect the Bank of Japan to hold its policy rate at one-point-two-five percent when its two-day meeting ends Friday. That follows last month's hike, which took the rate to a thirty-one-year high.
The number that frames the decision is September core inflation, which rose two-point-one percent from a year earlier, the highest reading this year. Inflation-adjusted wages rose one-point-five percent in August, the eighth straight monthly gain.
Every respondent also expects the bank to set the stage for a December or January increase. Thirteen see a terminal rate of one-point-seven-five to two percent by around mid-2027.
Why it matters is duration. Nine of the sixteen expect the ten-year Japanese bonds yield between three and three-point-one percent by year-end, from two-point-eight-zero-nine percent. Seven see the thirty-year reaching four to four-point-one, from three-point-eight-two-nine.
A hawkish hold lifts Japanese yields without any policy change, and that widens the pull on global duration. Higher Japanese bonds yields make Japanese institutional money less eager to leave home.
That matters for Treasuries, for euro-area sovereigns, and for every market that has leaned on Japanese demand at the long end. The yen is the second channel.
Nine respondents see it between one-fifty-seven and one-fifty-eight against the dollar over the next month. A weaker yen keeps import costs elevated and feeds the inflation case the bank is being asked to answer.
Watch Friday's language on the pace of further adjustment, and watch the vote split. A single dissent would move December pricing more than the hold itself.
The core fact again. The Bank of Japan is expected to stand still Friday at one-point-two-five percent, and to signal that the next move comes within months.
