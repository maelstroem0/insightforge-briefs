LEAD STORY
Meta raised the floor on its twenty twenty-six capital spending guidance and reported negative free cash flow for the third quarter. Meta says it now expects capital expenditure of one hundred thirty-five to one hundred forty-five billion dollars this year, up from a one hundred thirty billion dollar floor.
That's a fifteen billion dollar lift at the bottom of the range — and it comes alongside negative free cash flow of two point one two billion dollars. Revenue of sixty-four point six billion dollars beat the sixty-three point three seven billion dollar consensus. The beat didn't matter.
Shares fell seven and a half percent on Thursday, and the company's five-year credit default swaps hit all-time highs. Guidance moved at the floor, not the ceiling — the part of a range that signals conviction rather than optionality.
Here's why this is bigger than one quarter. The AI trade is moving from chips to capital structure. A hyperscaler funding data centers out of cash flow is one risk. A hyperscaler funding them with debt, against a spending floor that keeps rising, is another.
The read-through runs to data-center suppliers, to power and cooling names, and to every neocloud borrowing against the same demand curve. It's also a conditional demand test. If hyperscaler capex discipline tightens, the order book behind AI compute gets repriced before the earnings do.
Nothing in the quarter says AI demand is falling. Everything in the quarter says the bill for serving it lands on the balance sheet first. Meta's own answer is Muse — whether advertising revenue from its AI products can close the gap the spending opens. Watch the fourth-quarter capex line, and any change in how the company funds it.
So: Meta lifted its spending floor, posted negative free cash flow, and the market priced the balance sheet rather than the revenue beat.

SECOND STORY
Turning to Treasuries, where the safe-haven bid did the work. Yields posted their biggest one-day drop since the 2023 banking crisis, and the ten-year closed at a one-month low. The benchmark slipped back below five percent. The trigger wasn't a soft inflation print.
It was the same oil price that has Brent above one hundred twelve dollars a barrel — read this time as a tax on growth rather than a lift to inflation expectations. Tech jitters added to it. Money leaving crowded AI positions needed somewhere to sit, and the deepest market in the world took it.
P G I M's Tipp describes the move as a take-back trade, not an all-out recession fear. That distinction matters. If it's a take-back trade, positioning normalizes and yields stabilize. If it's the start of a growth repricing, there's further to go, and every rate-sensitive sector reprices with it — utilities, real estate, long-duration equities.
This is the mirror image of Thursday's equity tape. The same AI spending doubt that hurt stocks helped bonds, which is not how an inflation scare behaves. For a multi-asset investor, that's the signal worth isolating: the long end is trading growth risk right now, not price risk.
That matters for positioning. A curve that rallies on growth fear is a different trade from one that rallies on disinflation. And note the pairing — crude at a five-month high and Treasuries rallying together. The next test is whether yields stabilize here or extend the decline.

THE ROUND-UP
In Japan, core inflation hit two point one percent in September, its highest this year and above the Bank of Japan's two percent target. The core-core measure, which strips out fresh food and energy, ran at two point four percent. Markets read it as cementing a December hike. The yen firmed to about one hundred fifty-six to the dollar.
In India, Jio Platforms' thirty thousand two hundred thirteen crore rupee offering — the country's largest ever — was oversubscribed five point five seven times by day two, with the institutional portion booked thirteen point two nine times. The grey market implies a twenty-one percent listing gain. Twenty-seven thousand five hundred crore rupees go to debt repayment.
In Washington, the Treasury proposed a rule expanding outbound investment restrictions on China, Russia and North Korea, adding hypersonic systems, supercomputing, biotechnology and artificial intelligence to the covered list. It applies only to investments above fifty thousand dollars, and it excludes stock purchases.
In China, DeepSeek raised about fifteen billion dollars — roughly one hundred billion yuan — with C A T L and Tencent among the largest investors. That's two days after Washington ordered federal agencies to strip DeepSeek's technology from their systems. A China listing could follow as early as next year.
On the corporate side, the US railroads all posted higher profit and revenue. Norfolk Southern's revenue rose fourteen percent to three and a half billion dollars on three percent more volume. Union Pacific's domestic intermodal volume rose thirteen percent, and C S X raised its revenue growth forecast to high single digits.
Norway's two point three trillion dollar sovereign wealth fund — the world's largest single investor — posted a third-quarter loss of two hundred seventy-eight billion kroner, about twenty-nine point seven billion dollars. Fixed income fell three point five percent. Equities returned half a percent.
And Moderna and Merck reported that their melanoma vaccine plus Keytruda cut the risk of recurrence or death by forty-two percent over two years. Eighty-two percent of the vaccine group stayed recurrence-free, against seventy percent on Keytruda alone. Moderna still closed down about eighteen percent after the data leaked early.

THE OUTLIER
Nvidia's Jensen Huang told a Berlin audience that neocloud demand is off the charts — then named the constraint: there's a scarcity of capital. He flagged power allocation in Europe as decisive for firms like Nebius and Mistral. Nvidia has committed nearly ten billion dollars in Europe. The signal is that AI infrastructure growth is now financing-limited and power-limited, not demand-limited. Watch financing terms and grid allocations.
