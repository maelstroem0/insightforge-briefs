LEAD STORY
French Prime Minister Sébastien Lecornu says France faces a risk of financial crisis, on the eve of presenting a 2027 recovery budget. It's the starkest language yet from a government fighting to hold down its own funding costs.
The evidence is already on the tape. French harmonised inflation ran at three-point-four percent in September. And the spread between French and German ten-year yields has widened to a level last seen in 2012.
The budget itself leans on local government. The plan seeks more than five billion euros of savings from local authorities, according to Challenges. Vanguard has flagged French debt-sustainability concerns, according to Le Figaro.
The channel that matters is the spread. A wider O A T-Bund spread pressures French sovereign debt and French bank funding costs. It also weighs on the euro against the dollar.
And it complicates the European Central Bank's path, at a moment when energy is lifting headline inflation across the bloc.
France is the euro area's second-largest sovereign market. When its premium widens, other high-debt issuers in the bloc get repriced against German bunds. European banks then carry a funding-cost question into the next earnings season.
The near-term trigger is political. Socialist deputies have signalled a censure motion against the government. Le Monde reported high National Assembly absenteeism in recent votes, which makes every headcount uncertain.
So watch the censure motion and the French-German spread together. Those two lines tell you whether Paris is buying time, or losing the argument with its creditors.
The core fact stands. Lecornu's crisis warning, inflation at three-point-four percent, and a widening O A T-Bund spread. France's fiscal risk is now a global duration story, not a local one.

SECOND STORY
Elsewhere, the global bond rout pushed the US ten-year Treasury yield above five-point-three percent — a twenty-four-year high, and the biggest quarterly jump since 1994.
This is a global move, not a domestic one. Sovereign yields have climbed across markets this quarter, and the US leg is simply the loudest.
The transmission into household credit is already visible. The thirty-year fixed mortgage rate reached seven-point-three percent, its highest since November 2023. Purchase-loan applications are down more than ten percent versus a year earlier.
For equities, the arithmetic is unforgiving. A higher risk-free rate compresses the present value of every distant cash flow, and it hits long-duration growth names hardest.
Guidance like Micron's can lift a sector even as the discount rate presses on it. Both forces are live this morning.
There's a second channel, and it's fiscal. Higher yields raise the interest burden on every sovereign that has to refinance, which is why the long end is now a fiscal story as much as a monetary one.
What to watch is a short list. September payrolls, then the thirty-year auction. Those two decide whether the ten-year holds above five-point-three percent, or extends further.
