LEAD STORY
The Wall Street Journal reports that illicit Iranian funds are flowing through US dollar clearing accounts, exploiting a gap in the American financial surveillance system. The scale is large. The reporting describes billions in Iranian money passing through US banks via correspondent accounts.
That creates an enforcement dilemma for Washington. Cut off the accounts, and dollar clearing tightens for everyone. Leave them open, and a sanctioned state keeps touching the US financial system. Neither option is cheap.
The likely response is regulatory tightening on correspondent banking and dollar clearing. That raises compliance costs for US and foreign banks alike. It could also restrict dollar-clearing access for certain jurisdictions, which would reach directly into trade finance flows and sanctioned-country currencies.
For investors, the transmission runs through banks and through the dollar itself. Any regime that makes dollar access scarcer strengthens the currency's leverage — and fattens the compliance line item at global banks. Watch for Treasury or congressional action on correspondent account rules. The report lands weeks after Washington's declared financial offensive against Iran, and it suggests the surveillance gap sits at home, not abroad.
So the core fact again: billions in illicit Iranian funds are transiting US banks through dollar clearing accounts, per the Wall Street Journal — and the fix may reshape how the whole world touches the dollar.

SECOND STORY
Turning to Tokyo. Japan's national government is expected to fully compensate local governments for revenue they lose from a consumption tax cut. That makes the cut a genuinely significant fiscal stimulus — and the center covers the cost.
The Japan Times reports the plan is meant to boost domestic demand and ease political pressure on the ruling coalition. And the timing is explicit. The paper says: "The national government is expected to present the plan at a meeting with local governments to be held shortly."
This is the third consecutive week we've tracked Japan's fiscal expansion, after budget requests topped one hundred thirty trillion yen with defense at a record. Now a tax cut on top. Consumption gets support. The fiscal position gets worse.
The market consequence lands in JGBs. Full compensation means the state absorbs the revenue hole, which points to more issuance at the long end. Japanese yields have already been repricing — the Nikkei dropped more than two percent over the past week. When the financing details are published, watch Japanese bonds issuance volumes and ten-year yields. That's where this plan gets priced.
