LEAD STORY
The United States has launched Operation Economic Outcast, an effort to cut Iran off from the global financial system.
The Iranian rial now takes more than two million to buy one dollar on the open market. That is a record low.
Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says, "Let there be no ambiguity as to the position of the United States. An economic engagement of any kind with this murderous regime will expose those responsible to the full reach of American power."
The sanctions widen across five sectors: digital assets, technology, gold, aviation, and shipping. They hit nearly sixty entities, individuals, and vessels.
Iranian oil exports have fallen from about two million barrels a day before the war to four hundred thousand barrels a day by mid-August. Food inflation is running above one hundred percent, and unemployment is rising.
China's imports of Iranian crude fell to five hundred thirty-four thousand barrels a day in August from eight hundred twenty-three thousand in July. The I M F expects the Iranian economy to contract more than five percent this year.
Iranian security chief Mohsen Rezaei says, "not a drop of oil will leave the Persian Gulf and the Strait of Hormuz."
The economic squeeze is meant to force Tehran to negotiate after nearly six months of conflict. But the threat to Hormuz keeps a floor under crude.
Brent traded near ninety-three dollars and W T I crude near eighty-six on Monday, both down about two and a half percent. The real risk is not today's oil price; it is the supply chain behind it.
Watch W T I crude support near eighty-nine dollars and any move by Washington to target banks. Operation Economic Outcast is now the main driver of oil and Gulf risk.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the long end of the Treasury market. The Treasury has doubled its buybacks of ten- and thirty-year bonds to at least four billion dollars over the next quarter.
The thirty-year yield fell six basis points to five-point-two-one-six percent after touching five-point-three percent last week, its highest level in nearly two decades.
Stanley Druckenmiller calls the move dangerous suppression. He says, "Every basis point of artificial yield suppression is a subsidy to procrastination."
The Treasury may tap its nearly one trillion dollar General Account to expand the buybacks further.
The ten-year yield fell more than four basis points to four-point-six-nine percent. Housing starts fell twelve-point-four percent last month, and the S and P 500 lost one-point-four percent last week.
Semiconductors fell more than five percent in that stretch. If Nvidia does not bring new buying with Wednesday's results, one strategist says he will raise the caution flag.
The risk is now explicit: fiscal policy is managing the most important price in the world. If the buyback support fails, the thirty-year could retest five-point-three percent and pressure risk assets.
Watch five-point-three percent as resistance. The Treasury has put its credibility on the thirty-year, and that is now the main trade in rates.
