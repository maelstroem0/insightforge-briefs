LEAD STORY
The thirty-year Treasury yield climbed to five-point-six-two-one percent — its highest since June 2002. That's the number that governs this morning, and it landed while the market was trying to talk itself out of an October rate rise.
New York Fed President John Williams says there is no need for urgency to raise rates again after September's move. Futures had priced an October hike as high as seventy percent. After Williams, that slipped to roughly fifty-one and a half percent.
The ten-year pushed to five-point-two-nine-three percent. That happened even as oil fell and consumer confidence dropped. So the long end rose while the growth data softened. That's the combination that matters.
When that happens, it isn't the policy rate doing the work. It's term premium — the price investors demand to hold duration. And term premium lands hardest on the cash flows sitting furthest out. Long-duration equities. High-multiple growth. Credit.
There's confirmation in the data. The Conference Board's consumer confidence index fell to eighty-one point nine, against eighty-nine expected. Job vacancies came in at seven point two two eight million, just under the seven point two seven million expected.
Equities weakened. Small caps weakened more. Everything that needs a lower discount rate to justify its valuation got marked down instead.
The long end has been repricing for weeks now, and the direction hasn't changed. Five-point-six-two-one percent is a 2002 number — a level most investors currently working have never had to price against.
Watch Wednesday's P C E print, then the October FOMC. A hot inflation reading re-arms the hike trade and pushes the long end again. The thirty-year at five-point-six-two-one percent, highest since 2002, is the level every other asset now prices against.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Middle East. US and Iranian negotiators held separate talks through mediators, and both sides left without movement. Iran has tied reopening the Strait of Hormuz to three concessions: release of frozen funds, lifting of oil sanctions, and an end to the naval blockade of Iranian ports.
Meanwhile, the physical market has largely healed already. JPMorgan data show Middle East crude shipments rebounded to seventeen point five million barrels a day — ninety-eight percent of pre-war levels.
Products tell a different story. Flows of diesel and gasoline are running at three million barrels a day, just fifty-eight percent of normal. Kpler put Hormuz clearance at ten thousand five hundred ninety-one kilobarrels a day on Saturday, against a pre-war baseline of seventeen thousand one hundred thirty-three.
So crude is moving. Refined products are not. That gap is where the freight economics live, and it's why diesel has stayed stubborn at the pump.
The risk premium and the supply reality are now decoupled. Tanker and Gulf logistics operators are being paid on flows that have recovered. The barrel is being priced on a threat that hasn't materialised yet. Iran has again threatened regional energy infrastructure.
Two things to watch. First, whether product flows close the gap toward the crude number. Second, whether any retaliation turns the threat back into a price.
The talks stalled. The barrels are flowing at ninety-eight percent. Both facts are true this morning, and the market is trading the second one.
