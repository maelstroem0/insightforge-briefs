LEAD STORY
Brent crude climbed about three-and-a-half percent on Friday to pass a hundred and seventeen dollars a barrel. That is its highest level since April.
It eased to just over a hundred and sixteen dollars by the close, still up about two-point-seven percent on the day.
The move followed Iranian threats to attack oil facilities, undersea cables, data centres and AI facilities in the Gulf, if a new American or Israeli offensive is launched.
For scale, Brent traded near seventy-two dollars a barrel in February, before the Iran war began. It touched a hundred and twenty-six dollars by the end of April.
US gasoline is now four dollars and twenty-nine cents a gallon, up forty percent from a year ago. Brown University researchers put the extra fuel spending at a hundred and forty-two billion dollars this year.
That is the transmission line for a portfolio. Higher energy costs keep headline inflation sticky, and sticky inflation keeps the Federal Reserve's hold case in view.
For equities, the split is straightforward. Energy producers collect the higher barrel; transport and consumer names pay for it at the pump.
Gasoline at four dollars and twenty-nine cents is a visible tax on household budgets heading into the November third midterms.
The threat itself is conditional. Iran ties a strike on Gulf infrastructure to a new offensive.
That is the tension in the cross-asset tape this morning. Crude is bid, government bonds are bid, and the inflation math is unsettled.
What to watch is the April peak at a hundred and twenty-six dollars, and whether Brent holds above a hundred and sixteen into the next session.
So the fact to carry into the weekend: Brent passed a hundred and seventeen dollars on Friday, its highest since April, as traders priced the risk of renewed strikes on Iran.

SECOND STORY
Also this morning, the rhetoric behind that crude bid has a name.
President Donald Trump, speaking at a Florida rally, revived the threat of renewed US military action against Iran, saying the country may launch "another little Epic Fury."
The war is nearing its eight-month mark.
He also floated intervention in Cuba, suggesting US ships could stop fifty yards off the island.
Iran answered that it could target Gulf undersea cables and American data centres if the United States attacks.
The shipping data is already deteriorating. Bloomberg cited regional naval organisations and maritime security consultants on at least twenty-three attacks on vessels in or near the Strait of Hormuz in the first two weeks of October.
At least five of those involved damage to engine rooms.
Cable risk carries its own arithmetic. Mauldin says capacity routed through the Red Sea accounts for about three percent of global international demand and around ten percent of traffic travelling between regions.
The consequence is a premium, not a shutdown. Escalation risk is a conditional input for shipping volatility, Gulf digital-infrastructure risk premia and defense demand.
The market has a calendar to price here. The fear is a campaign that resumes after the midterm elections on November third.
Nothing in the rally line is agreed or scheduled. It is a threat, and it stays conditional.
Watch the Hormuz incident count, and whether the rhetoric turns into action rather than position-taking.
The core fact: the president put renewed strikes back on the table, and Iran answered with a threat to the cables and data centres that carry Gulf traffic.
