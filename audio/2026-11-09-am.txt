LEAD STORY
Texas has just put a brake on the AI buildout. Its governor-elect has promised to ban new data-center approvals until the legislature acts, after Democrats swept the midterms and took Texas statewide offices and both chambers of Congress.
The number that matters: Texas holds one-fifth of the planned 253-gigawatt US data-center pipeline.
The votes explain the politics. Down the ballot, voters in ten states decided 41 local measures on data centers — and in 40 of the 41, they favored limits on development. In an N B C exit poll, 76 percent opposed new data centers in their communities.
The market answered fast. Within days, 3.2 trillion dollars of AI-related stock market wealth was wiped out, as investors bet the election winners would slow the buildout.
Why it matters beyond Texas. On P J M, the grid running from Illinois to New Jersey, the independent market monitor puts data centers at 6.3 billion dollars of the latest auction's 16.4 billion in capacity charges.
Across the last four auctions, that's 29.4 billion dollars, or 46 percent.
So the risk isn't demand. It's timing. A permitting pause in the largest single state pipeline transmits to utility and grid-equipment orders, and to the capacity charges data centers themselves pay. For investors, that's a capex-timing risk on AI infrastructure and grid-capacity names, not a demand call.
The pressure is broad. Data Center Watch counted 833 opposition groups across 49 states by March 2026, up from 396 at the end of 2025.
In the October A B C News/Washington Post/Ipsos poll, 57 percent of Americans called home energy and utility costs unaffordable, up from 45 percent in February.
What to watch: whether the Texas legislature writes a moratorium or a framework, and whether other states copy the language. Either way, the approval — not the chip — is now the gate on Texas data-center capacity.

SECOND STORY
Turning to China, where the price picture has split in two.
China's October producer-price index rose 5.7 percent from a year earlier. Economists polled by Reuters expected 5.3 percent. It's the fastest pace since June 2022.
Consumer prices went the other way. October C P I slowed to 0.7 percent, missing the 0.8 percent expected and down from 0.9 percent in September. It's the first deceleration after two straight months of increases.
Core inflation, which strips out food and energy, came in at 0.8 percent, down from 1 percent the month before.
The cause is two-sided. Factory-gate prices accelerated for a third straight month on sharp rises in mining and raw materials prices, and on very strong external demand.
Consumer prices were pulled lower by weak domestic demand and falling food prices.
Producer prices matter for the world because China exports its costs. When factory-gate prices run this hot, the pressure lands in industrial supply chains and in the goods that flow into Europe and the United States.
Consumer prices matter for Beijing because they measure whether households are spending. At 0.7 percent, they aren't spending enough to let companies pass costs on.
The read-across: accelerating producer prices signal external cost pressure. Slowing consumer prices signal a domestic economy that still can't generate pricing power. That combination is the deflation debate in one release.
Equities took it in stride. The C S I 300 gained 0.56 percent after the data.
What to watch: whether producer prices keep feeding through to export prices, and whether Beijing answers weak consumer demand with more stimulus. Industrial commodities and Chinese equities are the first places that shows up.

THE ROUND-UP
To the wider brief. Houthi missiles targeted Riyadh on Sunday, and debris damaged the Aramco Tower in the King Abdullah Financial District. The Saudi Energy Ministry reported minor damage and no injuries.
The Saudi-led coalition said it intercepted and destroyed two ballistic missiles fired toward the capital. A strike on the airport in October killed 13 people and wounded more than 300.
Japan spent a record 50 billion dollars in a single day during July to prop up the yen, the Wall Street Journal reported — part of an aggressive campaign that included rare joint action with the United States.
The supplied reporting doesn't state the yen's response, the campaign's total size, or the levels involved. Watch dollar-yen positioning for the read.
In Washington, Democrats are preparing a first-100-hours legislative blitz and investigations aimed at Trump's tariffs, after their midterm landslide. Senator Ron Wyden called repeal of tariffs on Canada an obvious priority.
A suit filed October 20th targets 50 percent tariffs on Canadian goods. The administration has already been ordered to refund 166 billion dollars in tariff revenue collected under a scrapped policy.
In Egypt, the government raised industrial gas curtailments for fertilizer and methanol plants to 33 percent, and notified Ezz Steel of a 30 percent supply cut — about 30 million cubic feet a day.
The trigger is L N G. The Asian benchmark J K M climbed roughly 36 percent month-on-month to more than 34 dollars per million British thermal units at the end of October, from about 25 dollars a month earlier.
A single spot cargo now costs close to 120 million dollars, and the government has deferred five of the 15 cargoes contracted for November.
And in Brussels and London, the EU and UK are in the final stages of a deal to link their carbon markets, almost six years after Brexit.
Negotiators have resolved emissions caps, supply controls and free permits, leaving only derivatives treatment outstanding ahead of the November 20th leaders' summit.
UK December carbon closed at 64 pounds 81 a ton on Friday. Benchmark EU allowances were at 86 euros 55. Linkage would narrow that gap and cut C B A M costs for steel, cement and fertilizer exporters.
