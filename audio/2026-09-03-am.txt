LEAD STORY
Iran attacked the Saudi-owned supertanker Sidr in the Strait of Hormuz late Monday, killing two Filipino sailors. A second tanker, the South Korean-owned Senegal Prosperity, was hit as well. It's the sharpest escalation yet against commercial shipping since the war began in late February.
The number that matters: commodity vessels crossing Hormuz fell to four on Tuesday, against a ten-day average of about thirteen. Brent crude has surged more than eight percent over three sessions, topping ninety-five dollars a barrel. West Texas Intermediate sits below ninety-one.
There is a counter-current. US Energy Secretary Chris Wright says more than seventeen million barrels of oil transited the strait on Monday, a record since the war began. In his words: "With or without Iran, oil and gas will flow out of the Arabian Gulf region and it's happening."
The consequences are now global. European gas hit its highest price since 2023 on Tuesday, with storage at lows last seen in 2013. Tokyo's Nikkei fell two-point-nine percent. Dennis Kissler of B O K Financial says the escalation should keep support under the market, but both Washington and Tehran are looking for off-ramps — and peace talks could deflate prices quickly.
So to restate the core fact: tanker traffic through the world's most important oil chokepoint has cratered to four ships a day, and Brent has broken ninety-five dollars. Watch two things. The transit count, and the E C B on September tenth, where markets price a twenty-five basis point hike as energy shocks feed eurozone inflation.

SECOND STORY
Turning to North America. President Trump has imposed fifty percent tariffs on roughly twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods after talks collapsed in mid-August. He also signed an executive order renaming Lake Ontario to Lake America on August twenty-seventh. Canada retaliates with tariffs of twenty-five to fifty percent on hundreds of US products starting Monday, September eighth.
American voters aren't with him on either move. A Reuters-Ipsos poll finds fifty-seven percent oppose the tariffs, with only twenty percent in support. Sixty-three percent oppose the renaming — and forty-three percent of Republicans are against it too. Forty-six percent say the United States is responsible for the trade war.
Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney wants the taunts to stop. "When the Americans stop doing memes, stop throwing shade, stop trying to be tough and start being serious about having those discussions, we can have those discussions." The White House, through spokesperson Taylor Rogers, calls Canada "a bad neighbor by ripping off our country for years."
The market consequence lands in autos and cross-border supply chains. Retaliation effective Monday hits hundreds of US goods, and Canada's central bank is already warning the conflict feeds inflation. Position accordingly: favor U S M C A-diversified manufacturers over Canada-exposed names into Monday's implementation.

The Nikkei dropped two-point-four percent, and Bitcoin slipped three.

THE ROUND-UP
Germany has formally blamed Russia for the explosive drone attack on a Ukrainian munitions plane at Leipzig airport last month, closing the last Russian consulate in Bonn. Saboteurs also knocked out two substations — four thousand two hundred megawatts — spiking power prices above six hundred euros a megawatt-hour. European defense and grid-hardening stocks are the obvious read.
At the G20 in Asheville, China blocked the finance declaration targeting export overcapacity, forcing a chair's statement. Nineteen nations, including Russia, approved the text. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent says "the fact that nineteen countries have wished to tackle this problem testifies to its scale." The tariff wall around steel, EVs and solar keeps hardening.
Enterprise AI is broadening past chips. Snowflake surged twenty-two percent after hours on earnings of sixty-two cents a share against forty-five cents expected, and raised its product revenue forecast to six-point-oh-seven billion dollars. Its CoCo coding agent now serves over nine thousand one hundred accounts. H P E's profit quintupled on AI server demand.
US private payrolls rose just thirty-eight thousand in August, the weakest since January and below the forty-seven thousand consensus, per A D P. Manufacturing shed seventeen thousand jobs. The pressure on the Fed now collides with an oil shock — and Friday's official payrolls print is the hinge.
The Bank of Canada held its main rate at two-point-two-five percent, warning the escalating trade conflict with Washington, in its own words, "threatens to add to inflation" — days before Canadian retaliation begins.
California's wildfire liability reform collapsed in the legislature. P G and E fell twenty percent this week, Edison International twenty-one, and P G and E's chief executive Patti Poppe cut two billion dollars from 2027 capital spending, to eleven-point-four billion.
And a structural milestone in Beijing's energy strategy: China's installed solar capacity reached one thousand two hundred eighty-six gigawatts at end-July, overtaking coal for the first time. Renewables supplied nearly seventy percent of new capacity this year — a direct hedge against imported oil in wartime.

THE OUTLIER
One number worth sitting with: this bond sell-off is everywhere at once. US ten-year Treasuries above four-point-eight percent, gilts at five-and-a-quarter — a post-2008 high — Bunds at their highest since 2011, and Japan's ten-year over three percent for the first time in thirty years. Watch Friday's payrolls: a strong print extends the rout, and long-duration sovereigns remain the trade to avoid.
