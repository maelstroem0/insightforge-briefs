LEAD STORY
Federal Reserve Chair Kevin Warsh used his first Jackson Hole address to signal a rate hike may come as soon as September. Traders now put the odds at roughly sixty percent. That is the single biggest repricing of policy expectations since he took the chair.
The speech itself was blunt. Warsh called inflation preoccupying — personal consumption inflation running three point seven percent year on year, sixty-five months above the two percent target. He said financial conditions are not restrictive. Markets heard a chair prepared to tighten into a slowdown.
The bond market did the heavy lifting. Two-year yields surged twelve basis points to four point three five six percent, and the ten-year pushed past four point six nine. Gold fell three percent as higher real yields bit. Japan's five-year yield hit a record two point one nine five percent, as American tightening exports itself abroad.
Why it matters: this is a hawkish turn priced into every risk asset at once. Short-dated Treasuries and the dollar are the winners. Emerging market bonds, gold, and rate-sensitive equities pay for it. If the ten-year breaks four point seven cleanly, the long end could accelerate beyond what a single hike justifies.
Watch the September FOMC. Between now and then, every inflation print and every labor number gets read through one question — does Warsh pull the trigger? Restating the core: Warsh's Jackson Hole debut moved a September hike to sixty percent odds, and the two-year at four point three five six percent says the market believes him.

SECOND STORY
Turning to energy, and a deal that redraws the supply map. President Trump announced a hundred-year concession giving the United States fifty-five percent control of a joint venture over seventeen Venezuelan oil fields — sixty-five billion barrels of proven reserves, backed by more than a hundred billion dollars of private investment led by Chevron.
Trump called it, in his words, "the biggest oil deal in world history." On Truth Social he wrote that the United States has, quote, "secured majority U.S. control of more than 65 BILLION BARRELS of proven Oil Reserves in Venezuela, at no cost to the American Taxpayer." Venezuela's interim President Delcy Rodriguez confirmed the agreement, calling it historic.
The scale is the point. Sixty-five billion barrels is roughly one-fifth of Venezuela's proven reserves, and the deal comes as US gasoline sits at four dollars nine cents a gallon and the Strategic Petroleum Reserve is at its lowest in forty years. Chevron plans to lift its Venezuelan output fifty percent by the end of 2028.
For investors, this is resource nationalism going American. Heavy-crude refiners benefit first as Venezuelan barrels ramp. OPEC+ cohesion is the loser — a hundred-year American-controlled concession is a structural addition to supply outside the cartel. Bearish the oil price over time, even as the Iran war keeps the near curve volatile.
Watch the ramp, not the press conference. Trump says the transaction more than doubles American oil reserves and will substantially lower gas prices. The fields need years of investment to deliver. The first test is whether Venezuelan output — about one point one million barrels a day in July — actually accelerates under the new structure.

MARKETS CHECK
Let's get to the markets — all figures as of Friday's close, August twenty-eighth. Equities rallied into the weekend: the S and P 500 up one point two percent, the Nasdaq up one and a half, the Dow up one and a half. The vix fell more than nine percent.
Rates barely moved after Thursday's repricing — the two-year was flat, the ten-year down two basis points. The dollar index added three tenths of a percent. Gold gained two point one percent on the day, though the week told a harsher story. Brent crude slid four point four percent. Bitcoin was up about two percent.

THE ROUND-UP
Now the sweep. France's fiscal credibility is fraying. Second-quarter G D P was flat, after a revised first-quarter contraction, and inflation accelerated to two point four percent in August. Labour Minister Jean-Pierre Farandou signaled pension de-indexation touching four hundred billion euros of annual spending — while guaranteeing the poorest stay indexed. Fitch affirmed France at A-plus but projects debt at one hundred twenty-two point seven percent of G D P by 2028. Watch the O A T-Bund spread into Moody's October review.
The Iran war marked six months, and the tactics have changed. Admiral Brad Cooper, commanding US Central Command, said the Strait of Hormuz's international shipping lanes are now free of Iranian sea mines. Twenty to thirty tankers transit nightly — roughly half of pre-war volume. Trump says he is, quote, "not in a hurry," pivoting to sanctions. Oil volatility stays bid even as the risk premium deflates.
Europe's frozen Russian money is back on the table. The Netherlands, Poland, Spain and Sweden want roughly two hundred ten billion euros in immobilized Russian central bank assets on the EU Foreign Affairs Council agenda September first and second — reviving a plan Belgium blocked over legal damages claims. Ukraine's foreign minister Andrii Sybiha says it is, quote, "only fair and logical to use Russian assets instead of European taxpayer money."
AI is running on borrowed money. Nvidia reported nearly sixty billion dollars in quarterly profit and sits inside more than seven hundred fifty billion dollars of AI investment — while Lambda alone borrowed a billion from J P Morgan to buy Nvidia chips for Microsoft. The AI debt market is on track for four hundred ten billion dollars this year. Circular financing is the cycle's fault line.
Wheat is a war trade now. The grain jumped twelve point one percent this week to seven hundred eighty-four cents a bushel — a three-year high, up more than fifty percent this year — as Black Sea strikes damaged Russian export infrastructure. Corn settled up five and a half percent on the week. Food inflation now passes straight into emerging market CPIs.
Canada just posted its fastest growth in over three years — three point three percent annualized in the second quarter, per Statistics Canada. The catch: Trump's threatened fifty percent auto tariffs cloud every quarter that follows, and Ottawa readies twenty billion dollars in retaliation.
And PayPal loses its takeover premium. Advent International and Stripe walked from a fifty-three billion dollar bid at sixty dollars fifty a share, over valuation and regulatory complexity. The stock dumped as much as fifteen percent premarket. Large-cap fintech M and A just got colder.

THE OUTLIER
One contrarian thesis to file away. The Wall Street Journal's Greg Ip argues an accord to revalue China's yuan, quote, "may be the only solution" to the flood of cheap Chinese exports distorting world trade. A Plaza-style deal would be a regime change: bullish emerging market currencies and non-China exporters, bearish Chinese mega-exporters. Watch the secondary-sanctions threat on Chinese banks — that is the leverage that could force Beijing to the table.
