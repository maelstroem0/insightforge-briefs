LEAD STORY
The United States says the Strait of Hormuz is now free of Iranian sea mines, six months into the war with Iran. Twenty to thirty tankers are moving through the southern channel every night, carrying nine to ten million barrels. That is roughly half of pre-war volume.
CENTCOM commander Admiral Brad Cooper says the sweep is complete. Quote: "Internationally recognized sea routes in the strait are free of Iranian sea mines." More than two hundred mine-like objects were cleared, eleven of them actual mines. Only two percent of ships were hit in the last month.
President Trump is calling the strait, in his words, "open" — and says he is "not in a hurry" to end the conflict. The administration is pivoting from military pressure to economic siege, with sanctions taking the lead as concerns grow over depleted American munitions stockpiles.
Iran is absorbing the damage. The I M F expects its economy to shrink five-point-four percent this year, with inflation above eighty percent. Washington has extended secondary sanctions to Egypt's Banque Misr. Iran's council secretary, Mohsen Rezaei, says the strait cannot be separated from the broader conflict, and that Washington must prove its seriousness first.
Why it matters: mine clearance is near-term bearish crude, but a sanctions blockade keeps a hundred-dollar spike on the board if Tehran retaliates against Gulf states. Transit is still thin — five to seven vessels a day against one hundred thirty pre-war — which supports tanker rates. Watch Chinese banks next. If Washington sanctions the institutions clearing Iranian crude, the whole sanctions architecture escalates again.
So the picture is this: the mines are gone, but half the traffic hasn't come back. The strait is open. The war is six months old and moving into its economic phase.

SECOND STORY
Also this morning: President Trump has signed a one-hundred-year concession giving the United States fifty-five percent control of sixty-five billion barrels of Venezuelan oil. The deal spans seventeen strategic fields, backed by one hundred billion dollars of private investment. Trump announced it on Truth Social, writing that the United States has entered, quote, "The BIGGEST O I L D E A L IN W O R L D HISTORY!"
Interim President Delcy Rodriguez confirmed the agreement and calls it a historic one. She says the investments will contribute to Venezuela's recovery, hemispheric energy security, and, in her words, greater balance in international markets. Caracas expects more than two hundred billion dollars in taxes over the life of the venture.
The stated goals are ambitious: double American oil reserves, refill a Strategic Petroleum Reserve that has fallen below three hundred million barrels — its lowest since the early nineteen-eighties — and push down gasoline prices. A gallon of regular is at four-oh-nine today, against three-twenty-one a year ago. Chevron plans to lift Venezuelan output fifty percent by end of twenty-twenty-eight, and Halliburton is in talks.
For investors: this is bullish heavy-crude refiners like Valero and P B F, and bearish OPEC-plus cohesion. Venezuelan barrels ramping into a half-open Hormuz press Brent. Watch Riyadh and Baghdad — their response to a century of American-owned supply decides whether the cartel's pricing power survives.
