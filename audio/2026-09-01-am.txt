BILLBOARD
Two prices set the tone overnight — what it costs to borrow, and what it costs to stay sanctioned. I'm Iris, and this is The ApexQuantix Briefings. It's Tuesday, September first. Kevin Warsh's hike odds hit sixty-two percent as the ten-year tops four-point-seven — and US-Iran strikes send Brent back above ninety. Plus, a bruising tape.

LEAD STORY
The Fed has fully repriced toward a September rate hike. Market odds of a hike this month reached sixty-two percent, up from roughly thirty-eight percent just a week ago.
Chair Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech did the damage. Two-year yields rose fourteen basis points to four-point-three-seven percent. The ten-year sits at four-point-seven-three, its highest since two thousand seven. The thirty-year approached five-point-three-four.
Warsh was explicit. He said: "We must be confident that underlying inflation is moving to our objective, clearly and at sufficient speed. Otherwise, we have work to do."
There's a counterweight. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent pushed back, saying: "It is my belief that we've seen a supply shock, and traditionally you don't raise into a supply shock unless you see second- or third-order effects."
Gold told the story. Spot gold fell half a percent to about four thousand four hundred thirty-five dollars an ounce — despite holding a ten percent gain for August on the Treasury's buyback plan. At M K S P A M P, Nicky Shiels calls it a tug of war between a dovish Treasury and a hawkish Fed.
Why it matters: a September hike reprices the whole curve. That squeezes leveraged credit, growth equities, and non-yielding assets like gold. Rate-sensitive sectors carry the brunt into the FOMC.
The core fact again: two-thirds of the market now prices Warsh hiking in September, with the ten-year at four-point-seven-three. Watch this week's inflation prints — they decide whether that hike lands on the twentieth or slides to year-end.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Persian Gulf. The United States and Iran exchanged direct strikes again on Sunday, ending a month of relative calm. US forces hit two rocket launchers on Iran's Larak Island, and Iran retaliated against US bases in Jordan.
The oil market moved first. Brent crude surged three-point-seven percent to around ninety-one dollars a barrel. W T I crude advanced two-point-eight percent to eighty-six fifty-nine.
Equities followed lower. The S and P 500 fell about a third of a percent. The Nikkei dropped roughly one percent, and the Kospi fell more than one.
The stakes sit in one waterway. The Strait of Hormuz carries about a fifth of global oil, and visible commodity transits fell to just five vessels a day over the weekend.
President Trump escalated the rhetoric on Truth Social, writing: "Iran is officially a Failed Nation. IT I S D E A D!"
From Motilal Oswal, Siddhartha Khemka framed the read-through: "Brent crude rose 3.7 percent to around US91 dollars/bbl after fresh US strikes on Iran, reversing the recent easing and bringing inflation, the rupee and external balances back into focus."
Why it matters: every dollar of crude feeds straight into the inflation prints Warsh is watching. That tightens the loop between Gulf escalation and Fed policy — and import-dependent Asian equities and E M currencies wear the risk premium.
Watch Hormuz shipping rates this week. Any disruption through the chokepoint is the tail that takes Brent well past one hundred.

MARKETS CHECK
Let's get to the markets. Two-year Treasury yields led the tape, up ten basis points as of Tuesday, at four-point-three-four percent. The ten-year added five basis points. The S and P 500 was up about half a percent, and the Nasdaq 100 gained one and a half. The dollar firmed about half a percent. Gold slid more than four percent. Brent eased nearly two percent. Bitcoin was little changed.

THE ROUND-UP
A quick sweep of the rest. German inflation rose to two-point-nine percent in August, just under forecasts, as energy prices jumped ten and a half percent. The ten-year Bund yield hit three-point-three-one — its highest since twenty eleven.
France is the concern next door. The ten-year O A T yield pushed above four-point-one-three, a seventeen-year high, as the deficit reached five-point-one percent of G D P and debt passed one hundred fifteen. Former Medef chief Pierre Gattaz calls the economic situation catastrophic.
India shrugged off the oil shock. Second-quarter G D P grew seven-point-eight percent, beating the seven-point-one the Reuters poll expected, driven by financial services, real estate, and IT. Barclays says consumer demand has stayed robust.
Beijing is on the move. Xi Jinping attends the S C O summit in Bishkek, makes his first state visit to Egypt in a decade, and heads to Washington on September twenty-fourth for a summit with Trump. Modi publicly urged Putin to end the Ukraine war, saying it must end for humanity's sake.
Amazon has a legal problem. The F T C and twenty-two states sued Monday, alleging the company secretly overcharged more than one-point-two million advertisers over twenty billion dollars since twenty nineteen by rigging its ad auction. Amazon denies the claims. The stock slipped roughly three percent.
Germany is set to name its suspect. Chancellor Merz says investigations into the August fourth drone attack on Leipzig airport are nearly complete, with Russia widely expected to be named. The response includes new sanctions and shadow-fleet action.
And a freight oddity: a shipping line paid a record five-point-three million dollars for a single Panama Canal slot, as drought cuts daily transits to thirty-two and Iran-war reroutes stretch waits to eleven days.

THE OUTLIER
One number for the housing desk. The average thirty-year mortgage rate hit six-point-eight-seven percent on Monday — the highest since June of last year. The war's oil pass-through did it, adding two hundred seven dollars a month to the payment on a median-priced home versus pre-war February. Homebuilders and mortgage REITs wear that squeeze first. Watch whether the Fed hikes into it this month — that's the difference between a grind and a stall.
