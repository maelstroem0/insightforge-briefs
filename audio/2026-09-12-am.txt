LEAD STORY
The Financial Times reports that Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent has failed to break what the paper calls the fever in the US bond market. Long-end yields stayed near multi-year highs.
That follows a technical attempt that didn't take. Earlier this month the Treasury tripled its ten-to-twenty-year buybacks, and long yields rose anyway.
The thirty-year Treasury yield was up eleven basis points as of Friday's close. The report supplied no auction result, no bid-to-cover ratio, no foreign-demand figure and no yield level, and it reported no market reaction to the Treasury's efforts.
What's being repriced, on the paper's framing, is fiscal credibility rather than growth. That reaches the long end first, then spreads into mortgages, corporate borrowing costs and long-duration equities.
For a global investor, the long end of the US curve is the world's discount rate. A credibility premium there isn't a domestic bond problem — it's a valuation input for risk assets almost everywhere.
The report doesn't tie the long end to a single cause. It describes a market that simply hasn't been talked down, and Bessent is the messenger. The market is asking about supply and about credibility, and the messenger sets neither.
The observable test is the tail at the next ten-year and thirty-year auctions, and the demand metrics inside those sales. That's where a failed message becomes a visible price.
So the core fact, stated again: Bessent has not broken the fever in the US bond market, and long-end yields remain near multi-year highs into the Fed's September meeting. Watch the auctions, not the speeches.

SECOND STORY
Turning to diesel. The Financial Times reports that US diesel has reached a record six dollars a gallon on the Iran supply shock.
Gasoline is the number drivers watch. Diesel is the number that moves goods — trucking, rail freight, and a large slice of airline operating cost.
The report doesn't disclose an as-of date, a crack spread, inventory levels or futures pricing. So treat six dollars as a reported record, not a live quote.
Why it matters: diesel feeds headline inflation faster than crude does, because it lands directly in the cost of moving things. It reaches trucking margins, rail economics and airline cost lines well before it reaches the consumer price index.
Crude is the input. Diesel is the invoice. And it arrives days before the Fed meets, with a hike, not a cut, on the table.
That's the pass-through channel the crude price only implies. For a multi-asset listener, it's the line item that turns an energy shock into a C P I problem. Records in products markets travel farther than records in crude, because there's no substitution for a truck.
Forward: the weekly E I A diesel inventory print is the test. Inventories decide whether six dollars holds or starts to mean-revert.
So the core fact once more: US diesel at a record six dollars a gallon on the Iran supply shock, with freight and transport costs carrying it straight into headline inflation.
