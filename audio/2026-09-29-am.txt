SECOND STORY
Turning to energy. QatarEnergy extended force majeure on L N G deliveries to Asia and Europe through the end of November, as cargo traffic through the Strait of Hormuz remains largely blocked.
Qatar has lost twenty-four billion dollars in sales over six months. Reuters calculations show exports fell by as much as ninety-six percent — eighteen cargoes shipped, against five hundred nine a year earlier, per I C I S data cited by Reuters.
Qatar is the world's second-largest L N G exporter, so this isn't a marginal supplier stepping back.
Europe is the pressure point. Gas Infrastructure Europe data show EU storage sites about seventy percent full as of September twenty-seventh, against a five-year seasonal average of eighty-six percent.
That gap — roughly sixteen points below normal — is what keeps the market tight. Germany's storage, the largest on the continent, is just over fifty-seven percent full.
Europe has also been pulling cargoes away from Asia. Vessel-tracking data compiled by Bloomberg show deliveries to Europe down only four percent from a year ago, against a thirty percent annual decline in early August.
European utilities are competing with Asian buyers for the same diverted cargoes.
The implication is straightforward. The supply gap keeps European gas and non-Hormuz L N G cargoes bid, and it lifts power and input costs for utilities and energy-intensive manufacturers.
A colder winter with Hormuz still shut is the scenario that pushes T T F through one hundred euros a megawatt-hour. A durable reopening is the relief valve.
