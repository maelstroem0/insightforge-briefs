LEAD STORY
Gold is trading below four thousand three hundred dollars an ounce, a day after touching a five-week low. Bullion was around four thousand two hundred ninety dollars, after falling more than one percent in the previous session.
Silver was down two-tenths of a percent at sixty-three dollars eleven cents, having dropped two percent the day before. The driver sits in the rates market: the ten-year Treasury yield briefly touched five percent, its highest since October 2023.
Traders are pricing between eighty-nine and ninety-two percent odds of a quarter-point Fed hike at this week's meeting. August consumer prices rose three-point-four percent year-on-year and three-tenths of a percent on the month, a reading that supports that pricing.
Together, a five percent ten-year and a firm inflation print lift the discount rate on an asset that pays no coupon. That's the mechanism behind gold's slide, and it's the same mechanism pressing rate-sensitive equity valuations.
Oil is the other leg. Brent crude jumped nearly four percent, keeping energy costs in the inflation math. The dollar added roughly two-tenths of a percent, and a firmer dollar makes dollar-priced bullion costlier for foreign buyers.
The Bloomberg Dollar Spot Index was steady, after rising four-tenths of a percent in the prior session. For an equity book, the transmission is direct: long-duration growth names answer to the same yield that competes with gold.
Safe-haven demand has not offset that math this week. Bullion ran hard through August, and this is the giveback. That is why a five-handle on the ten-year matters more than the metal itself.
The next test is the Fed's statement on Wednesday. Gold below four thousand three hundred dollars an ounce, at a five-week low, with the Fed the next word.

SECOND STORY
Also this morning, Saudi Arabia's East-West pipeline is still shut after last week's drone attack. The attack came by drone. The line runs twelve hundred kilometres across the kingdom, and it carried four to five percent of global oil supply.
It is the kingdom's land route around the Strait of Hormuz. Hormuz remains blocked, so both Saudi export outlets are effectively gone at once. Repairs are estimated at three to five weeks.
The line is Saudi Arabia's overland alternative to moving crude by sea through Hormuz. That is a supply shock with no rapid fix.
Brent traded at one hundred eight dollars sixty-five cents a barrel, up almost eighty percent this year, and up more than fifty percent from its July low of seventy dollars. West Texas Intermediate traded above one hundred one dollars.
German diesel hit two euros and forty cents a litre on Sunday. For markets, the split is clean: higher crude supports integrated energy earnings and squeezes energy-intensive margins. The euro-area consumer is the exposed end of that.
Freight, chemicals and the euro-area consumer absorb the rest. It also feeds the same inflation math the Fed and the E C B are fighting, and it lands the same week the Fed is expected to deliver a hike.
Watch the repair timeline — three to five weeks — and any signal on Hormuz reopening talks. Until the East-West line restarts, four to five percent of the world's oil supply is off the board.
For portfolios, energy equities and diesel-linked logistics carry the positive read; anything that buys fuel carries the negative one. Shut line, shut strait, and a repair clock measured in weeks.
