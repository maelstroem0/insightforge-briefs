LEAD STORY
The thirty-year fixed mortgage rate just posted its biggest weekly jump in four years. Freddie Mac reports it rose a quarter of a percentage point week over week, to seven-point-two-eight percent, as of Thursday.
That's the highest in nearly three years. The driver isn't housing. It's the Treasury market. The ten-year yield is up more than one and a quarter percentage points since February, and mortgage pricing follows the long end, not the policy rate.
The demand response is already visible. Mortgage applications fell six percent in the week ended September twenty-fifth. Adjustable-rate mortgages reached ten-point-three percent of applications, a signal that borrowers are stretching for affordability rather than paying up.
Capital Economics forecasts a six-point-two-five percent average by the end of 2027. That's a forecast, not a print, and it assumes the Treasury rout doesn't persist. If it does, homebuilder volumes, mortgage-origination revenue and household credit face further pressure.
For an equities investor, two channels are running at once. Discount rates just moved higher, which compresses the present value of distant cash flows. And housing-linked discretionary demand faces a genuine financing shock.
The mechanics matter. Thirty-year mortgages are priced off the ten-year and the mortgage-backed spread. So a fiscal term premium lands directly on a household's monthly payment.
This is the mortgage market's version of the same repricing pressuring long-duration sovereigns. It's now on the household balance sheet, with a lag of only a few days.
Watch Friday's payrolls, the next long-dated Treasury auction, and the thirty-year gilt yield in London. Each is an observable test of whether the long end has found a ceiling.
The core fact once more: the thirty-year fixed mortgage rate jumped a quarter point in a single week to seven-point-two-eight percent, the largest weekly move in four years, and the bond market wrote it.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the dollar. The dollar index was 102.08, set for a third consecutive weekly gain — a run last seen in May of 2025. The advance came as the global bond rout pressured the euro and Asian currencies.
Sterling traded at one-point-three-one-eight-seven. The New Zealand dollar slipped zero-point-two-two percent to fifty-five-point-nine-one cents, its lowest since November of 2025.
The transmission runs two ways. A firmer dollar tightens global financial conditions, because so much borrowing and invoicing happens in dollars. And it hits net energy importers twice — through the currency, and again because crude is priced in dollars.
That second channel is doing real work right now. Brent is above a hundred dollars a barrel, and the currencies buying it have weakened into this move.
The domestic test lands this morning. September payrolls are forecast to show the unemployment rate at four-point-one percent for a third straight month. A firm print reinforces the long end. A soft one gives the Fed room to stand pat.
Note what isn't happening. This isn't a panic bid for dollars. The equity tape is soft, but not disorderly, and gold is lower on the week.
Three straight weekly gains is a pattern this index hasn't strung together since May of 2025. This morning's payrolls print is the next test of it.
The core fact: the dollar index at 102.08, a third straight weekly gain, with the bond rout as the driver and energy importers as the pressure point.

THE ROUND-UP
Washington has sent a third aircraft carrier to the Middle East, adding as many as ten thousand troops, as the president weighs renewed strikes on Iran. Brent settled above a hundred and two dollars a barrel on Thursday and was at one-oh-two-sixty at zero-zero-twenty-two G M T. It gained around fourteen percent in September alone.
China has halted most October refined fuel exports as Beijing prioritizes domestic supply. PetroChina cancelled several gasoline and jet fuel cargoes, and Zhejiang Petrochemical scheduled no exports over the National Day holiday. Kpler estimates commercial diesel and gasoil stocks sit around twenty million barrels below pre-war levels.
That matters because distillates are roughly forty-one percent of European oil demand and about nineteen percent of US demand. Removing Chinese barrels from the market tightens an already thin balance.
The eurozone manufacturing P M I rose to fifty-two-point-nine in September from fifty-two-point-seven — the highest since May of 2022. The output sub-index hit fifty-three-point-six, a fifty-five-month high. But input and factory-gate prices rose faster.
Friday's print is expected to show eurozone consumer inflation accelerating to three-point-six percent from three-point-two percent in August. Resilient factories and faster prices is not a comfortable combination for the E C B.
C N B C reports the president could target three Federal Reserve governors, though removals may be harder than they appear. Nothing has been filed, no governors named, no timeline given. Any credible move would raise the term premium and test Fed-independence credibility.
Lynas Rare Earths agreed to buy Meteoric Resources in a six-hundred-seventy-two-million-dollar deal, gaining a foothold in Brazil's rare-earths industry. It's the latest move in a race to rebuild production outside China. Watch offtake agreements and permitting on the Brazilian asset.
G20 trade ministers ended two days of Milwaukee talks without consensus on industrial overcapacity or forced labor. The US trade representative said nearly all countries agreed action was needed, but that trade remedies are inadequate. The US-China Board of Trade still has no implementation timeline.
Ethiopia severed diplomatic ties with Eritrea on Thursday, giving ten Eritrean diplomats forty-eight hours to leave. Egypt declared an Ethiopian embassy counsellor persona non grata in return. No market pricing is attached, but Horn of Africa instability keeps Nile-dam and Red Sea shipping risk on the board.

THE OUTLIER
The yen finished the third quarter as the G10's best-performing currency, up three-point-three percent against the dollar, per Deutsche Bank data. That followed a joint US-Japan intervention and a twenty-five basis point Bank of Japan hike in September.
The transmission is carry-trade funding. A stronger yen and higher Japanese bonds yields raise the cost of the world's cheapest borrowing. Japan holds more than one-point-one trillion dollars of Treasuries, the largest foreign share. Watch whether Tokyo intervenes again.
