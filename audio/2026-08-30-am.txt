LEAD STORY
Kevin Warsh's Jackson Hole speech has pushed the market-implied odds of a September rate hike to fifty-eight percent, up from thirty before he spoke. The two-year Treasury yield hit four-point-three-four percent — its highest in a month. That's the number repricing the front of the curve right now.
The committee itself is split. Bianco Research puts the voting tally at five for a hike, five for a hold, and two unknown. Robin Brooks, the former chief economist at the Institute of International Finance, argues the speech wasn't a signal about September at all. Quote: 'Warsh had to sound hawkish yesterday. That wasn't a signal for a September hike, but about containing long yields, which rose sharply after his dovish performance on July twenty-nine.'
Either way, the consequences are already in the tape. Gold fell two-point-nine percent to around forty-five sixty-seven as real yields climbed. A stronger dollar is now a headwind for crude on top of easing Hormuz flows. Rate-sensitive equities and short-duration bonds are the exposed trades.
The calendar gives this a near-term catalyst. Governor Chris Waller speaks September third — the FOMC decision follows September sixteenth. Watch whether Waller confirms or pushes back on the hawkish framing. Odds of a hike by the October meeting already sit near seventy percent.
So the core fact again: September hike odds went from thirty to fifty-eight percent in one week, and the two-year sits at a one-month high. The Fed Chair said what he said. Now the committee votes.

SECOND STORY
Turning to Ukraine. A Russian drone strike on an ammunition depot near Kyiv has killed at least thirty-seven people, and President Zelensky has now ordered Ukraine's long-range drone output tripled to a thousand a day.
The strike hit the village of Myla on August twenty-eighth. Forty-two people were injured, including four children. More than fifty residential buildings and a care home were damaged, and over three hundred seventy people were evacuated. Zelensky blamed what he called terrible negligence and opened a criminal investigation.
Here's what he said about the depot itself. Quote: 'The first impact was on a place where ammunition was being stored that absolutely should not have been there – a depot of the defence forces.' The Prime Minister called it criminal negligence, noting a similar Kyiv-area depot strike in July killed twenty-two.
The broader picture is escalation. Zelensky has warned of a planned Russian ground offensive on Kyiv and Chernihiv, following a twenty-eight-hour combined strike. Both sides are heading toward mutual deep-strike campaigns, not a ceasefire.
For positioning, that's a risk premium story. European defense names — Rheinmetall, Thales — get support, as does gold. European equities and Russian assets carry more risk premium. Watch whether the drone-production ramp shows up in strike frequency over the next two weeks.

THE OUTLIER
One number worth a pause. Germany now has the most expensive household electricity in the G20 — thirty-five-point-six cents per kilowatt hour, more than double the group average of sixteen-point-three. Taxes, levies and grid fees are over sixty percent of the price.
The investor transmission is straightforward: chemicals, metals and autos face a structural cost disadvantage against French peers at twenty-three-point-three cents and the US at eighteen-point-three. Expect continued industrial relocation out of Germany.
The watchpoint is political — pressure for energy tax relief in Berlin. If relief comes, energy-intensive German names catch a bid. Until then, the underweight stands.
