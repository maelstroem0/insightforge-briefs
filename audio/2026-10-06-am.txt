LEAD STORY
The French ten-year spread to Germany has reached a fifteen-year high of about one hundred fifty basis points. It peaked at one hundred fifty-two on Friday and last traded near one hundred forty-five. That is the widest since 2011.
The repricing has already moved off the sovereign curve and into corporate credit. French investment-grade spreads have widened twenty basis points since the end of August. High yield is sixty basis points wider over the same stretch.
Banque de France governor Emmanuel Moulin warned that France risks being strangled by rising rates without consolidation. The French ten-year O A T yield sits near five percent, so that warning is not abstract.
Why it matters: French issuers now fund themselves in a wider market, and the backstop is absent. The E C B's anti-fragmentation support is not available to a member state under an excessive-deficit procedure.
The O A T-Bund spread keeps both legs by name for a reason. Germany is the leg being bought. The widening is happening on the French side, where the borrower pays.
So the channel is funding cost, not default risk. Banks, utilities and leveraged borrowers refinance into spreads that have already moved. The stress compounds the longer consolidation is delayed.
The sequencing is what matters. Sovereign spreads move first, corporate spreads follow, and the borrower sees it last in the cost of new debt.
What to watch is the 2027 budget negotiations. That is the next observable test. A poorly received auction or syndication would confirm the repricing rather than reverse it.
Restating the core fact: France's ten-year spread to Germany is at a fifteen-year high near one hundred fifty basis points. French corporate credit is already paying the difference.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Strait of Hormuz. The International Chamber of Commerce warns that gridlock there risks a global food crisis. Secretary-general John Denton says attention has focused on the resulting rise in oil prices.
What the world should be watching, he says, is food. That is the half of this chokepoint story that does not show up in the barrel price.
No figure was supplied with the warning. No commodity price, no freight rate, no food-supply number. So this is a stated risk, not a measured one, and it should be treated as conditional.
The transmission is a cost channel. Food-import-dependent economies, agricultural commodities and shipping all sit downstream of a strait that stays disrupted, and none of them reprice instantly.
Freight takes time, and so does the importer's decision to pass the cost along. That lag is exactly why a food effect is easier to miss than an oil effect.
The chamber's framing is that the world is watching the wrong number. Oil is the visible one. The delivered cost of food is the slower one.
For a portfolio, the read sits in agricultural commodities, in shipping economics, and in the import bills of economies with no domestic buffer.
Nothing here is priced yet. The measurable tests are Hormuz traffic, food prices and freight indicators. Easing in any of them would defuse the warning.
Restating it: the I C C says Hormuz gridlock risks a global food crisis, and the oil price is not the whole cost.
