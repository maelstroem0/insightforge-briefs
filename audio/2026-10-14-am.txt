LEAD STORY
Iran has targeted twenty-four tankers and other vessels around the Strait of Hormuz in two weeks. At least five of those incidents came in the past forty-eight hours. Officials put the war's monthly price tag at three billion dollars.
Two senior Iranian officials say the aim is to keep oil prices high through November's US midterm elections. Iran adopted the new tactics about ten days ago, adding unarmed spotters in small boats.
Before the war, the Strait carried about a fifth of global oil and L N G trade. That's the channel that matters. Strikes that keep transits thin hold a premium in crude, in freight rates and in tanker insurance.
It's a premium, not a shortage. No supply has been confirmed lost to these attacks alone. Six of those vessels were hit on Sunday alone.
Tanker economics improve when risk premia rise. The cost lands on charterers, and on refiners bidding for Gulf-delivered barrels.
The escalation continues a pattern. Hormuz attacks hit their highest weekly level of the war eight days ago, and transits fell to a two-month low.
That cost is now a key issue on the US campaign trail, in races that will decide control of Congress. Washington has said it will not strike Iran before the vote.
For a global investor the read-through is narrow and specific. Gulf freight and war-risk insurance first, crude differentials second, and the margins of Asian refiners that buy Gulf barrels third. None of that has been quantified in the reporting.
Watch the strike frequency. Another week at this pace keeps the premium bid. A pause lets it bleed.
The core fact again: twenty-four vessels targeted around Hormuz in two weeks, and a war running at three billion dollars a month.

SECOND STORY
Turning to China. Exports rose thirty-six point three percent in September from a year earlier, customs data showed Wednesday. That's the fastest pace since February, and it's an acceleration from twenty-five percent in August.
Shipments to the United States grew nearly thirty-nine point six percent, outpacing overall exports and lifting the monthly trade surplus to a hundred thirty-three point five billion dollars — the largest since January 2025. Imports climbed thirty-one percent, ahead of the twenty-five and a half percent consensus.
Car exports rose fifty-six point three percent to one point one eight million vehicles. Producer prices climbed five percent, the fastest in more than four years, on higher fuel costs.
The bellwether channel here is demand and pricing power. Export-led Chinese growth with reaccelerating factory-gate inflation feeds Europe's and Washington's trade retaliation debate directly.
Brussels is already pressing Beijing to rebalance. The two sides extended their rare-earth de-escalation deal this month and set a next meeting for March 2027.
There's a split worth naming. Domestic passenger-vehicle retail sales fell twenty-eight percent in September, a twelfth straight monthly decline, while exports of the same vehicles rose. Growth is being sold abroad, not consumed at home. The US-bound surge landed even as both Washington and Brussels pressed Beijing on trade.
Watch whether EU rebalancing measures move from review to proposal, and whether Chinese domestic demand closes the gap with the export side.
The core fact: September exports up thirty-six point three percent, US-bound shipments up nearly thirty-nine point six, and producer prices up five percent.
