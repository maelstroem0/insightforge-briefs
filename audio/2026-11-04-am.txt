LEAD STORY
Democrats have won control of the United States Senate and are projected to take the House, flipping Texas, Kansas, Iowa, Ohio, North Carolina and South Carolina.
Senate control comes with fifty-one seats. That is a working majority, and it ends two years of single-party control of the legislative agenda.
Exit polling reported by the networks found nearly ninety percent of voters called the high cost of living a major factor in their choice.
Sixty-two percent disapproved of President Trump's job performance. The Iran war, tariffs and the president's approval ratings all weighed on the result, according to the same reporting. Sweep odds on Polymarket stood at seventy-two percent.
The consequence is arithmetic, not sentiment. A Democratic Congress changes what can move through Washington. Fiscal packages, the debt ceiling and legislation touching AI data centers all take a different path now.
Healthcare subsidies and caps on credit-card rates move onto the live agenda. Both are direct revenue questions for insurers, pharmacy-benefit managers and card issuers.
Investors are repricing a regulatory overhang for AI data-center spending, for defense budgets and for card issuers, with healthcare and clean energy seen as the relative beneficiaries.
Nothing about that read is settled. A projected majority is not a certified one, and the House tally is still being counted.
What to watch is the final House count and any oversight agenda aimed at data-center capital spending.
For now, the fact is the sweep itself: the Senate has flipped, the House is projected to follow, and policy risk is back inside the price of AI infrastructure.

SECOND STORY
Turning to bonds. Treasury yields fell as investors crowded into safety during an election-driven sell-off in stocks.
The benchmark ten-year note yield dropped nearly five basis points to four-point-nine-zero-two percent. The two-year pulled back six basis points to four-point-five-five percent, and the thirty-year sat at five-point-two-nine percent.
The front end did the most work and the long end moved least. That shape — short rates lower, the thirty-year anchored — is the signature of a safety bid rather than a change in the growth outlook.
Equity futures took the other side of the trade. Contracts tied to the S and P 500 shed one-point-one percent, and Nasdaq-100 futures slid two percent.
The reported driver is the sweep itself. Investors sought shelter as the result raised the prospect of policy shifts touching the data-center buildout.
That is the second-order trade in this election. The same vote that caps fiscal legislation also introduces oversight risk to the AI capital-expenditure cycle, and duration is where that caution shows up first.
For credit, the question is whether oversight slows a buildout that has been funded increasingly with debt rather than cash flow.
The arithmetic underneath hasn't changed. US net interest costs have already set a record above a trillion dollars, and divided government doesn't rewrite that line.
Lower yields are a tailwind for duration and for agency mortgage-backed securities. The long end had already been easing off its highs through late October.
What to watch is the final House seat count, and whether the front end holds its bid if the labor data stays firm.
The core fact stands: the ten-year is at four-point-nine-zero-two percent, and the move lower came from the election, not from the data.
