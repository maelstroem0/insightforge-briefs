LEAD STORY
Pimco's chief investment officer says the ten-year Treasury yield could reach six percent for the first time since 2000. That's the top of a range that has already climbed nearly a hundred and twenty basis points this year.
The benchmark traded around five-point-two-nine percent, just under the five-point-three-four percent high it touched last week — its highest since 2002.
Pimco's Dan Ivascyn points to elevated oil prices, mounting concerns about US public debt, and technical pressure from hedge funds unwinding losing bond positions. He also warns that a move to five-and-a-half percent or higher could pressure risk-sensitive assets. The report gives no timing for the six percent level.
This matters because the ten-year is the discount rate under almost everything else. At five percent and change, it competes directly with equity multiples and with long-duration growth names.
It also sets the terms for mortgage and corporate credit markets that price off the curve. The emphasis in the report falls on oil and debt. As long as crude stays elevated, term premium has a reason to stay bid.
Watch one line and one number. The line is whether the ten-year holds below that five-point-three-four percent high or breaks above it. The number is the next inflation and auction data, where the market either accepts or rejects the six percent case.
The core fact, once more: Pimco's C I O says the ten-year Treasury yield could hit six percent, with the benchmark near five-point-two-nine percent after a hundred-and-twenty-basis-point climb this year.

SECOND STORY
Elsewhere this morning, OpenAI told investors its annualized revenue hit fifty billion dollars at the end of September. That's below the sixty-eight billion dollar figure widely reported last month.
The statement was confirmed by C N B C. A separate French report said OpenAI cut its revenue trajectory by twenty billion dollars — a figure that conflicts with the C N B C disclosure. Treat fifty billion as the confirmed number.
The market's reaction on Thursday was measurable. The Nasdaq Composite fell one-point-two-five percent, its biggest one-day drop since mid-August. Nvidia dropped two-point-nine percent, Advanced Micro Devices three-point-nine percent, and Oracle five-point-five percent.
The channel here is the AI revenue question, and it runs through credit as much as equity. AI-related corporate bond offerings have exceeded half of net investment-grade issuance for the year through August.
The S and P 500 technology sector's market-cap weight sits at roughly forty percent, above the dot-com peak. That combination means a revenue verification test doesn't stay inside one company.
It transmits through chip demand, through data-center capital spending plans, and through credit spreads on the paper that funded them. There's also a real gap in the numbers. Investors had been told sixty-eight billion. The company now says fifty.
Watch two things. First, OpenAI's next disclosed figure. Second, whether hyperscaler capital spending guidance holds through the next round of earnings. If it holds, this is a valuation problem. If it doesn't, it becomes a credit problem.
The core fact once more: OpenAI's annualized revenue was fifty billion dollars at the end of September.
