LEAD STORY
A Wall Street Journal survey of economists sees the ten-year Treasury ending this year at five point one percent. That's up from a four point four percent forecast in July — a seventy basis point revision in a single quarter. The survey attributes persistent borrowing costs to inflation, Federal Reserve rate increases and debt.
The ten-year has already climbed almost one hundred twenty basis points this year to five point two nine percent, near its highest since two thousand two. Junk bond yields hit seventeen percent this month. Higher long-end yields raise the discount rate for global equities and pressure long-duration assets. Rate-sensitive sectors — real estate, utilities, growth stocks — carry the heaviest burden.
One strategist in the survey expects the ten-year to approach six percent next year. That's not a consensus view, but it frames the risk. The move matters for everything from corporate refinancing to mortgage rates to equity multiples. Corporate borrowers face higher coupons when they roll maturing debt. Homebuyers face higher mortgage rates. Equity investors face a higher hurdle for discounted cash flows.
The same survey sees oil below eighty dollars a barrel by July — a forecast that sits awkwardly against a closed Strait of Hormuz and Houthi strikes on Saudi energy infrastructure.
Federal Reserve policy sits at the center of the survey's forecast. The Fed has already raised rates once and projects above four percent through 2027. Higher policy rates anchor the front end of the curve, but the long end is being driven by something else — term premium. Investors are demanding more compensation for holding duration risk. That's the compensation for lending long when inflation and fiscal risk are elevated.
The term premium has been rising as fiscal deficits swell. When supply rises and demand doesn't keep pace, yields rise. That's the dynamic the survey is capturing.
The survey's five point one percent year-end call is the number that matters — a full seventy basis points above July's consensus. Watch the next auction of thirty-year paper and the Fed minutes for any signal on duration extension.

SECOND STORY
Elsewhere this morning, Houthi forces in Yemen claimed four large-scale operations in twenty-four hours against Saudi Arabia. The targets included Riyadh and Dammam airports and two Saudi Aramco sites. Saturday's strike on King Khalid airport killed thirteen people and wounded three hundred nine. A Sunday strike killed one and wounded eleven. Houthi officials warned airlines off Saudi airspace.
The attacks mark an escalation in a conflict that has already disrupted Red Sea shipping for months. Saudi forces have run one hundred twenty-four air strikes since Saturday. B P, Exxon, TotalEnergies and Shell executives exited Riyadh's World Petroleum Congress. The Houthis said the operations would continue.
No Aramco production impact or oil price reaction has been reported. That's the key distinction. The strikes hit infrastructure, but the market has not yet priced a supply disruption. Insurance premia and freight rates are the first places that stress would show up. Watch those two gauges for confirmation.
A closed Strait of Hormuz compounds the risk. Iran's Supreme National Security Council declared the US reply to its seven conditions for reopening the waterway insufficient. That keeps the strait shut. Projectiles struck two outbound tankers on October tenth and eleventh, according to U K M T O.
The combination of a closed Hormuz and Houthi strikes on Saudi energy infrastructure creates a two-front supply risk. The market has not priced it yet — Brent is up, but not dramatically. That could change if insurance premia spike or freight rates jump.
