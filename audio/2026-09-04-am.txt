LEAD STORY
France's ten-year borrowing rate is now above four-point-two percent. That's the number that frames everything else in Europe this morning. On Thursday, France did manage to borrow thirteen and a half billion euros — but at a rising cost. Debt service is projected to reach nearly sixty billion euros in 2026. That's the price of the deficit before a single new policy is funded.
The backdrop is grim. Unemployment sits at eight-point-three percent and rising. Second-quarter growth was zero. The deficit is projected at five-point-nine percent of G D P in 2027 without adjustment. And eighty-two percent of French business leaders are pessimistic about the economic impact of the next president's policy — whoever wins. Eight months out from the April election, candidates are staking out positions. Hollande wants to raise the standard V A T rate from twenty to twenty-four percent. Le Pen has announced one hundred twenty-five billion euros in savings targets. And Mélenchon urges monetizing E C B-held debt.
Daniel Baal's verdict is blunt: the alert threshold has been reached — le seuil d'alerte est atteint. Prime Minister Lecornu, meanwhile, says the coming budget may in practice only cover the first half of 2027, leaving the rest to the next majority. Markets are reading that as fiscal paralysis with an election clock running.
Why it matters: the O A T-Bund spread is the cleanest divergence trade in Europe right now. Germany's composite P M I beat forecasts while France's slipped deeper into contraction. Long Bunds, short OATs, into year-end. The risk is political — a radical fiscal platform gaining traction in the 2027 campaign widens the spread further, not less. Watch the next auction and the draft budget, due by early October.
So the core fact again: France's ten-year yield is above four-point-two percent, and debt service is heading toward sixty billion euros a year. The bond market has already started pricing the election.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the metal. Gold jumped more than two percent to four thousand four hundred eighty-eight dollars an ounce on Thursday, pressing against the forty-five hundred mark. The driver: A D P private payrolls came in at thirty-eight thousand for August, well below the forty-seven thousand expected. That, plus dovish comments from Fed Governor Christopher Waller, cut the odds of a September hike to roughly a coin flip — from sixty-two percent before.
Waller says that if upcoming data confirms inflation pressures are cooling, he'd be inclined to argue for keeping rates steady at the next meeting. That's the sentence that repriced the front end. The dollar softened and yields eased off their highs, and gold caught the bid.
StoneX strategist Bob Haberkorn puts it this way: more and more traders are coming around to the idea that the Fed could do one rate hike, but there probably isn't much more they can do after that. His shop sees a possible move toward forty-six hundred. Central-bank demand adds a structural floor underneath — and Europe's repatriation flows, which we'll come to, reinforce it.
Everything now hinges on today's nonfarm payrolls print, expected to show roughly fifty to fifty-five thousand jobs added. A weak number and the dovish repricing accelerates — forty-six hundred is in reach. A strong print risks a sharp reversal from these levels. Long gold into the data is the crowded trade; size it accordingly.

THE ROUND-UP
Volkswagen's supervisory board has unanimously approved the largest restructuring in the carmaker's eighty-nine-year history. One hundred thousand job cuts by 2030 — about fifteen percent of the workforce — the model lineup halved, and four German plants phased out of auto production between 2031 and 2034. Shares closed nearly eight percent higher.
In the Arctic, Norway seized the Russian cruise ship Professor Molchanov off Svalbard on August thirty-first, to enforce a four-point-two-two billion dollar arbitration award won by Ukraine's Naftogaz. Russia calls it piracy and has lodged a formal protest. Naftogaz says it will pursue Russian assets around the world until it's paid.
Argentina's President Milei announced economic sanctions on firms developing the Sea Lion oilfield near the Falklands — one-point-seven billion barrels — plus a new naval base in Tierra del Fuego. His line: the winds of change favor our claim. Watch Rockhopper and Harbour Energy exposure.
Washington sanctioned Banque Misr U A E — Egypt's second-largest bank — for processing one-point-eight billion dollars for companies linked to Iranian shadow banking, routed through China's C I P S system. Treasury Secretary Bessent's warning was explicit: do business with Iran, and the US Treasury will put its full might against you. Bearish Egyptian sovereign risk.
US retail is bifurcating. Lululemon crashed fifteen percent after cutting full-year guidance for the fourth time — leggings sales down twenty percent — while Victoria's Secret raised its outlook on bra and Pink line strength. Same consumer, opposite direction.
Europe's winter buffer looks thin. German gas storage stands at just fifty-three percent fill, well below seasonal norms, and the one-year forward power price has surged to one hundred twenty-two euros per megawatt-hour — the highest since 2023. Bullish L N G exporters, bearish energy-intensive industrials.
And Broadcom lifted its AI chip outlook to roughly two hundred thirty billion dollars in revenue by fiscal 2028, citing committed capacity agreements — over ten gigawatts for Anthropic, five for OpenAI, three for Meta. Third-quarter AI revenue tripled to sixteen-point-seven billion, though soft the fourth quarter guidance pushed shares down after hours.

THE OUTLIER
One for the reserve-asset file. The Dutch central bank has moved eighty-six tonnes of gold — worth over eleven billion dollars — from New York and Ottawa to London, citing, in its own words, increasing geopolitical unrest. Governor Olaf Sleijpen says the transfer improves the tradability of the reserves: they expect never to need them, but resilience and preparedness require it. It follows France's repatriation of one hundred twenty-nine tonnes. The transmission is direct: European central banks are physically reducing US custody, a quiet vote of no confidence that structurally underpins the gold bid. Watch whether more euro-area central banks follow.
