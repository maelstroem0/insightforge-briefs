LEAD STORY
Fed Chair Kevin Warsh delivers his first Jackson Hole speech today, facing the most divided central bank in years. The stakes are unusually high. The P C E price index rose three-point-seven percent in July. Core inflation sits at three-point-three percent, and traders give a seventy-four percent chance of a quarter-point hike by December.
The committee itself is split. Three officials dissented for a hike at the July meeting. Cleveland Fed President Beth Hammack says: "I don't want to prejudge anything. But I believe now is the time to act." Kansas City's Jeffrey Schmid calls inflation "still stubborn and it's still sticky."
Warsh also faces pressure from the other end of Pennsylvania Avenue. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent is running a four-billion-dollar-plus buyback of long bonds, and critics say it blurs the line between Fed and Treasury. P G I M's Greg Peters says: "I have a very dim view of the Treasury's rationale and its tinkering. I think it's a self-limiting, self-defeating strategy."
Here's why it matters. The thirty-year yield sits above five percent, near levels last seen in two-thousand-seven. A hawkish surprise from Warsh could push it toward five-and-a-half percent, steepening the curve against Bessent's buyback. A dovish tone would ease financial conditions just as inflation runs well above target.
The dollar is near a one-week high, and Bitcoin trades around eighty thousand dollars — both ready to move on the speech. Watch the thirty-year yield and the curve into this afternoon. And keep the core fact in view: Warsh speaks Friday with inflation at three-point-seven percent and December hike odds near seventy-four percent.

SECOND STORY
Turning to energy. The United States is in advanced talks to take a direct equity stake in seventeen Venezuelan oil fields holding ninety billion barrels of crude. Venezuela, meanwhile, is weighing an exit from OPEC after six decades. The talks follow January's capture of Nicolás Maduro and Washington's takeover of Venezuelan oil sales.
The scale is significant. Venezuela holds three hundred three billion barrels of proven reserves in total, but currently produces about one-point-two-five million barrels a day. Roughly half a million barrels a day already moves from Venezuela to the US. A US official familiar with the negotiations says: "Calling this deal huge would be an understatement. It is massive."
The numbers come with a caution. Rystad Energy estimates one hundred eighty billion dollars of investment is needed over the next decade, and fifty billion just to keep production flat over fifteen years. David Goldwyn of Goldwyn Global Strategies says: "Incremental production is all we will see until the framework improves, electricity is restored, and the political picture becomes clearer."
The transmission is straightforward. A US equity stake in Venezuelan supply undercuts OPEC's control of pricing and pressures long-dated crude curves. It's also bullish for US refiners geared to heavy crude and for oilfield services. Watch Caracas's OPEC decision and the shape of any framework agreement for the pace at which barrels actually return.

The deal locks up open-source AI distribution, from GPUs to developer platforms, and leaves A M D and independent model platforms on the back foot. Antitrust scrutiny is the key risk.
