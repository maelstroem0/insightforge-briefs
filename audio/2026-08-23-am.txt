LEAD STORY
Iran is now letting Iraqi tankers through the Strait of Hormuz for the first time in six months. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent prepares to unveil what he calls the toughest sanctions in history on Monday.
The opening follows a six-month war that started February twenty-eighth and has choked the strait to twenty percent of its pre-war traffic.
The waterway itself is barely functioning. Only seven vessels passed through on Thursday, down from a pre-war norm of 130 to 140 a day. Brent crude sits near ninety-four dollars a barrel, and W T I crude is just above eighty-seven.
Bessent says the coming measures are 'the greatest coordinated economic isolation in the history of the world.' He will announce them at two p.m. Eastern on Monday, targeting Iran's oil exports and the Chinese customers who buy more than eighty percent of its shipped crude.
The Strait normally moves twenty million barrels of oil per day. That has collapsed to an eight-million-barrel daily average. The disruption is deepening crude supply risk.
Iran's economy is contracting at a six-point-one percent annual rate. Inflation is above eighty percent, and the currency has lost thirty percent this year. But Tehran is not backing down.
Iran's new security chief Mohsen Rezaei warns that any country joining Washington's economic war will be considered an enemy. The United Arab Emirates has suspended all trade and financial transactions with Iran.
The first Iraqi transits offer only marginal relief. Iraq produced about four million barrels per day before the war, and most of that remains stranded. President Trump views the Strait of Hormuz as American territory and says Tehran is not ready to make the right deal.
Watch Monday's sanctions announcement and whether Iran follows through on threats to target regional states. A further escalation that hits tankers could push Brent toward one hundred twenty dollars. For now, oil, gold and volatility remain bid, and the risk premium is not fading.

SECOND STORY
The United States has imposed fifty percent tariffs on twenty billion dollars of Canadian goods after last-minute demands sank trade talks late Friday.
The move invokes Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930. The levies took effect Saturday at just after midnight Paris time and cover roughly five percent of Canadian exports to the United States.
Canadian Prime Minister Mark Carney says Canada will match those tariffs 'dollar for dollar to protect our workers and businesses.' He calls the US action an attack and a trade war.
Ottawa's retaliation will begin September eighth, targeting US steel, dairy and electronics. The two economies traded three hundred seventy-six billion dollars in goods and services in just the first half of this year.
R B C estimates the direct effect at four tenths of one percent of Canadian value added and employment. The average US tariff on Canadian goods is now five percent, up from a tenth of one percent before twenty twenty-five.
Supply chains for autos, steel and agriculture face immediate disruption. The Canadian dollar is the first pressure valve, followed by earnings from North American manufacturers.
The US share of Canadian exports fell to seventy-two percent last year from seventy-six percent. Ottawa's exports to non-US markets rose seventeen percent, showing where the pivot is heading.
US wine exports to Canada fell seventy-eight percent year over year, a three hundred fifty-seven million dollar loss, and US spirits exports dropped seventy percent.
This is the fourth escalation in a week after three days of pauses and hints about reviving the Keystone X L pipeline. Canada's economy is already slowing, with R B C projecting G D P growth of one point one percent in twenty twenty-six. Watch the September retaliation date and any further White House tariff threats.
