LEAD STORY
The ten-year Treasury yield rose more than thirteen basis points, to five-point-one-two-five percent.
The two-year, the maturity most sensitive to Fed decisions, jumped more than fifteen basis points to four-point-eight-seven-nine percent. The thirty-year added ten basis points, to five-point-four-four-nine.
The two-year had already surged seventeen basis points on Tuesday alone — its biggest one-day move since April of last year.
Traders are now pricing a December Fed hike at one hundred percent odds, and two more quarter-point increases by June.
The repricing follows hawkish commentary from Fed officials, sticky and broadening inflation, and oil adding to the pressure.
West Texas Intermediate gained more than two percent to close at eighty-eight dollars seventy-four a barrel. Brent rose more than three percent, to ninety-five dollars eighty-two.
Financial conditions are tightening through two channels at once — the price of money, and the price of energy.
That combination pressures rate-sensitive housing and technology, and it raises the hurdle rate for every refinancing and every capital project that depends on the bond market.
For households, the ten-year is the benchmark for mortgages, auto loans and credit card debt. At five percent, it is a direct drag on the rate-sensitive economy.
Housing is already answering. Existing home sales fell to their lowest level in more than sixteen years last month.
For equities the arithmetic is unforgiving. A higher discount rate lands hardest on long-duration growth, which is why technology and smaller companies carry the most sensitivity. It also gives fixed-income allocators a genuine alternative to stocks.
The next test is today's October producer price index, expected to show a four-tenths-of-a-percent rise, down from a one-point-three percent surge in September.
Then the December FOMC decides whether this is a quarter point, or something larger.
The core fact, once more: the ten-year is back above five percent, and the market has stopped debating whether the Fed hikes again.

SECOND STORY
Turning to Nvidia. The board accepted co-founder Jensen Huang's resignation as president, chief executive and board member, effective immediately.
Chief financial officer Colette Kress becomes interim chief executive. Harvey C. Jones, a longtime director, takes the newly created role of board chair.
The stock fell four-point-three-four percent Thursday, after falling as much as fourteen percent intraday. Roughly five hundred fifty billion dollars of market value has gone in five days.
So the company at the center of the AI boom now has no permanent leader, and no public succession plan.
That matters well beyond one ticker. Nvidia's chips are the scarce input for every large AI build-out, and its balance sheet has been a financing source for the ecosystem.
The company has carried roughly ninety-nine billion dollars of equity investments in frontier labs, neoclouds and infrastructure companies.
A leadership vacuum at that supplier is a conditional risk to AI-infrastructure demand, to neocloud financing, and to export-control policy toward China.
Context for the scale: Nvidia shares had closed above the four trillion dollar market cap mark since July of last year.
The next tests are quarterly results on November seventeenth, and the appointment of a permanent chief executive.
To restate it: Nvidia's board removed its founder, its finance chief is running the company, and the market has taken half a trillion dollars out in a week.
