LEAD STORY
AI infrastructure credit is repricing, and the equity market is only starting to catch up. Oracle's five-year credit default swap spread has widened to two hundred eighty-eight basis points from one hundred eighty-six on September eleventh.
The yield on CoreWeave's nine-point-six-two-five percent bond maturing in 2032 has reached fourteen-point-eight percent.
Those are the two clearest prints of a funding channel that's tightening around the debt-funded AI buildout. Global AI-related debt issuance fell to twenty-three billion dollars in September, from one hundred thirteen billion in June. That's less than a quarter of June's pace.
Gartner warns that three-point-five trillion dollars of AI infrastructure spending could be impaired by 2030. Gartner's concern is the financing behind that spending, which assumes a cost of capital that no longer exists.
The equity tape is already answering. A Goldman Sachs basket of regional AI hardware stocks fell eleven percent last week, its steepest decline since mid-July.
Nan Ya Plastics, the best performer on the M S C I Asia-Pacific Index this year, tumbled twelve percent from a record high.
And the forward estimates are bending with it. Consensus earnings growth for global AI stocks is expected to moderate from around one hundred percent in 2026, to thirty percent in 2027, and twenty-two percent in 2028.
The transmission runs through three channels. Borrowers with near-term maturities face refinancing at yields that price distress. Equipment suppliers see order books tied to financing, not to end demand. And equity holders in the same complex carry a discount rate that just moved against them.
The core fact, restated. Oracle's five-year credit default swap sits at two hundred eighty-eight basis points, up from one hundred eighty-six on September eleventh. CoreWeave's 2032 bond yields fourteen-point-eight percent.
What to watch is whether Oracle's spread holds below three hundred fifty basis points this week. Above that, the funding channel is the story, not the model.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Strait of Hormuz, where the war's shipping toll has set a new high. Twenty-four vessels were hit in the two weeks to October eleventh, according to Financial Times analysis.
That's the most intense period of attacks in the entire war, and almost double the second-worst stretch, when thirteen ships were hit in the first two weeks of March.
Brent is holding above one hundred dollars a barrel. The I C E Brent futures contract for January 2027 trades at one hundred two dollars and fifty-nine cents. The June 2027 contract is slightly lower, at ninety-two seventy-one. December 2027 trades at eighty-four seventy-five.
The curve is telling you the market expects relief eventually, not soon.
The physical market is tighter than the headline. Only two-point-nine million barrels transited the Strait on October eleventh. The International Energy Agency is releasing more than twenty million barrels of diesel, with prices at all-time highs.
The I C E low-sulfur gasoil contract for November trades above one thousand four hundred four dollars a ton. December 2027 gasoil is a thousand and one.
The cost is landing on Europe. The European Union is thought to have paid an additional one hundred billion euros on oil and gas so far this year. Since 2022, extra energy costs have surpassed nine hundred billion euros, even as total demand has fallen.
Storage is the next pressure point. Germany holds Europe's largest capacity, is roughly half full, and is expected to miss its official eighty-percent target this year.
For context on the move. Between late September 2025 and 2026, oil prices rose sixty-five percent, diesel forty-five, and jet fuel one hundred twelve.
Restating the core. Twenty-four ships hit in two weeks, and Brent above one hundred dollars. Watch tanker traffic through the Strait, and the gasoil curve, for whether the distillate squeeze widens into winter.
