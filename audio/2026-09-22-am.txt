LEAD STORY
Brent is holding just above a hundred dollars a barrel, after four straight sessions of selling knocked more than nine percent off the front of the curve.
The November contract gained twenty-two cents, or twenty-two hundredths of one percent, to trade at a hundred dollars and fifty-seven cents a barrel in early trade.
That follows a slide that took Brent as much as four-point-eight percent lower, and West Texas Intermediate down more than six percent, to near ninety-five dollars.
Look at the barrels. Tanker tracking data shows Saudi Aramco loaded about fourteen million barrels of crude onto seven supertankers inside the Gulf on Sunday.
Saudi shipments through Hormuz have averaged two-point-nine million barrels a day, against seven hundred thousand in August.
That physical recovery, plus hopes for US-Iran diplomacy at the U N General Assembly this week, has eased the immediate shortage trade.
The market is trading the flows, not the headlines about them.
The consequence is not a lower cost of energy. Crude is still up more than sixty-five percent this year.
And the product side hasn't eased at all. Diesel notched a fresh high above six dollars and fifty cents a gallon at US pumps, even as diesel futures fell more than four percent and gasoline futures about two percent on Monday.
Falling crude with record diesel keeps product cracks wide against the barrel, and that is the channel that feeds freight, trucking and consumer prices.
Risks cut both ways here. Saudi barrels are moving through the strait itself, and that is the route that stays the chokepoint.
The next test is whether the diplomacy produces a concrete deliverable at the U N this week, or whether the geopolitical premium comes back into the barrel.
For now, Brent is holding at a hundred dollars and fifty-seven cents.

SECOND STORY
Turning to the Fed. Chicago Fed President Austan Goolsbee says the Fed may have to cause economic pain to bring inflation down.
His words, delivered in London on Monday: "It's going to be painful."
That is a blunter register than Fed Chairman Kevin Warsh used last Wednesday, when the central bank raised its benchmark rate to around three-point-nine percent.
It was the first increase in three years.
The stated aim is to cool business and consumer demand to match diminished supply, and bring inflation back to the two percent target.
Supply disruptions from tariffs and from Iran-war oil prices have pushed inflation higher.
Goolsbee says the Fed has little option left except to raise rates, given persistent supply-side pressure.
The consequence for portfolios is direct. If the committee holds its two percent target above employment, rate-sensitive sectors face further tightening.
The channels are the front end of the curve, the dollar, and long-duration equity cash flows. The dollar is the cleanest read on that.
None of that is confirmed by an observed repricing yet, so treat the transmission as conditional. What is confirmed is the disagreement.
Goolsbee's remarks contrast with the chairman's framing, and that gap is what traders will price over the coming weeks.
For a listener holding duration, that language matters more than the hike itself. It tells you the committee is not looking for an exit.
Watch the next round of Fed speakers, and whether guidance firms toward another move or toward a pause.
