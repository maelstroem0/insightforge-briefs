LEAD STORY
Houthi forces fired ballistic and cruise missiles and drones at Riyadh and at an Aramco facility in Yanbu on Saturday, setting an Aramco fuel tank ablaze near King Khalid International Airport.
FlightRadar24 listed the airport at its maximum disruption index — five point zero.
That is the first alert of its kind for the Saudi capital since the escalation with the Houthis began in July. Saudi air defences intercepted a ballistic missile over Riyadh.
Houthi spokesman Yahya Sarea said the strikes answered Saudi air operations over Yemen during the past week.
This is a first for this war. Houthi fire has hit Saudi energy infrastructure before. It had not reached the capital since the escalation began.
The immediate read-through is energy and freight. A fuel depot is a physical asset, not a financial one. But markets price what could be hit next.
Gulf equity risk premia, insurance costs for tankers calling at Saudi ports, and crude volatility all sit on the same trigger.
Yanbu sits on the Red Sea, the alternate exit for Gulf crude when Hormuz is contested.
The United Nations said on Friday the renewed fighting had displaced more than one hundred twelve thousand people. Nearly three thousand have fled across the sea to Djibouti.
What to watch from here: shipping traffic through Hormuz and Bab el-Mandeb, and any widening of Saudi crude-loading delays.
That's the channel from a missile over Riyadh to a price on a barrel. Nothing trades on a Sunday, so the market's answer waits for Monday.
The core fact. Houthi missiles and drones reached Riyadh and Yanbu on Saturday. A fuel depot at King Khalid airport burned, and the airport hit its maximum disruption index. The war has now touched the capital.

SECOND STORY
Turning to energy. The chokepoint in this war has moved.
CENTCOM says Gulf allies have moved more than one billion barrels of crude through the Strait of Hormuz in recent months.
U.S. mine-clearing and naval protection pushed Hormuz oil and L N G flows to a six-month high.
So the strait, for now, is functioning. The bottleneck is downstream.
The Wall Street Journal reports refineries are now the main chokepoint for global energy supplies.
Shipping is adding twenty-six dollars per barrel to the cost of delivered oil. A supertanker shortage is developing, and that threatens long-haul flows.
The mechanism is narrow and mechanical. Mine-clearing reopened the water. It did not add refining capacity or tanker hulls.
Crude can move. Product can't move fast enough. That's why the refining gate matters more than the strait right now.
For investors, the distinction decides the trade. Refiners with feedstock flexibility and tanker owners capture the freight and crack economics. Refiners without that flexibility wear the cost.
That is not a uniform energy bet. It splits the sector.
Yesterday this brief noted Saudi Aramco had zeroed European October allocations. Today's detail explains the mechanism: this is a shipping and refining constraint, not a shortage of crude in the ground.
Iran maintains the Strait of Hormuz is closed. CENTCOM's number says more than a billion barrels moved through it. A claim and a measurement can both be true at once.
The watchpoint is Bab el-Mandeb transit volumes and tanker rates.
One fact to hold. Shipping now adds twenty-six dollars a barrel to delivered oil, and the supertanker fleet is tight. The energy risk has migrated from the water to the refinery gate.

THE OUTLIER
Spot container rates from China to the US East Coast have reached ten thousand nine hundred forty-eight dollars per forty-foot box — more than quadrupling since the Iran conflict began on February twenty-eighth, per Xeneta, and closing on the January 2022 record of eleven thousand nine hundred.
